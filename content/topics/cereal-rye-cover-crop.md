Cereal rye (_Secale cereale_), also called winter rye, is a cover crop planted in fall after corn, soybean or another crop is harvested. It survives the winter, grows quickly in spring, and is ended (terminated) before or soon after the next crop is planted.[^elmquist-2026] What a rye cover crop can do depends on how much it grows: a little rye holds nitrogen, more protects the soil, and a heavy stand suppresses weeds. This page explains how much growth each goal needs, how weather drives that growth, and how to manage rye for your goal.

## Biomass for each goal

Biomass is the dry weight of the rye's above-ground growth, in pounds per acre. UW–Madison researchers have set biomass goals for the main reasons growers plant rye:[^elmquist-2026]

- **Holding nitrogen:** 1,000 to 2,000 lb/acre. At 1,000 lb/acre, rye can take up about 25 lb/acre of nitrogen that might otherwise leach. Up to 1,000 lb/acre, there's no need to change your nitrogen plan; in manured fields with more rye than that, UW–Madison suggests reducing the manure's nitrogen credit.
- **Erosion and phosphorus runoff:** about 1,500 lb/acre, which cut sediment and phosphorus losses in half in a simulated heavy rainstorm. Depending on the field and the storm, you may need more or less.
- **Weed suppression:** 3,500 to 4,500 lb/acre. In UW–Madison trials, about 2,500 lb/acre halved waterhemp growth and 3,500 lb/acre halved giant ragweed growth, but halving the number of weeds that come up took about 4,500 lb/acre for waterhemp and 4,250 lb/acre for giant ragweed.[^chudzik-werle-2026]

Even heavy rye doesn't control weeds by itself, so keep a residual herbicide in the program.[^chudzik-werle-2026] The only way to know your own rye's biomass is to clip, dry and weigh a measured area.[^elmquist-2026]

## How weather drives growth

Rye grows when it's warm enough, so its growth follows growing degree days from planting to termination. Planting earlier gives it more of them.[^elmquist-2026] Rye planted after corn silage has a long fall to tiller and root before winter. Rye seeded from mid-October on makes little growth before winter but can still survive it, though an open, snowless winter with extreme cold can occasionally kill it.[^elmquist-2026] Most of the biomass comes in spring, and delaying termination adds to it: rye ended at soybean planting or later produced about 35 % more than rye ended two weeks before.[^elmquist-2026] Rain matters too.[^elmquist-2026] Researchers have predicted rye's late-season biomass from its early growth, degree days, planting and termination dates, sunlight and rainfall.[^huddell-2024]

## Managing rye for your goal

**Planting.** Drill at about 60 lb/acre, ¾ to 1½ inches deep; raise the rate 5 to 20 % if you broadcast. After an early harvest such as corn silage, about 40 lb/acre can be enough.[^elmquist-2026]

**Termination timing.** About two weeks before planting is a reasonable start if you're new to cover crops.[^smith-2022] The choice trades biomass against risk to the next crop:

- **Before corn,** end rye early. Iowa State recommends terminating at least 10 days before planting corn: rye roots can carry _Pythium_ seedling blight to the corn, especially in cold, wet springs. Above about 2,000 lb/acre of rye, corn usually needs extra nitrogen. Corn planted into living rye ("planting green") yielded less than corn planted after termination at most sites in a multi-state study.[^robertson-licht-2026][^elmquist-2026]
- **Before soybean,** high-biomass rye fits better.[^chudzik-werle-2026] In Wisconsin research, planting soybean green and ending the rye within a few days of planting had little effect on yield in years with normal rain.[^smith-2022] For weed control, rye about 3 feet tall is a good time to terminate.[^chudzik-werle-2026]
- **In a dry spring,** end rye early, especially if no rain is forecast before planting.[^smith-2022][^chudzik-werle-2026]

**Termination methods.** Glyphosate (WSSA group 9) at standard burndown rates controls rye.[^wssa-hrac][^take-action-herbicides] Spray while it's actively growing, with temperatures of about 55 °F by day and 40 °F at night for three days before and after. Roller-crimping works only once rye reaches full flowering (anthesis), usually late May in Wisconsin; crimping earlier often fails to kill it.[^smith-2022] Check with your crop insurance agent: in Wisconsin, cover crops generally must be terminated before the cash crop emerges.[^smith-2022]

For details, see UW–Madison's [Biomass Thresholds for Cereal Rye Cover Crop Goals](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/articles/biomass-thresholds-for-cereal-rye-cover-crop-goals/) and [Spring Cover Crop Termination Considerations](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/articles/spring-cover-crop-termination-considerations), and Iowa State's [Terminating cover crops ahead of corn](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/post/terminating-cover-crops-ahead-corn).

## AgWeather's models

The [winter rye cover crop biomass model](/models/rye-biomass) estimates a fall-planted rye's biomass through the spring. It grows with growing degree days since planting (base 32 °F, capped at 95 °F, by the single-sine method), and it is lower after a later planting or a wetter fall (rain from planting through December 31). It rates the estimate Low below 2,000 lb/acre, Moderate from 2,000, and High from 4,500, the weed suppression goal. Its Moderate level starts above UW–Madison's goal for erosion control (1,500 lb/acre), so a Low estimate near 2,000 may already meet that goal.[^agweather-rye-biomass][^elmquist-2026]

The model is an unpublished fit to Wisconsin data, carried over from the Crop Risk Tool, and it reproduces that tool exactly. We don't know its error or the planting dates and rainfall its data covered, and it hasn't been compared with biomass measured in fields, so treat its numbers as a rough guide and clip your own rye before relying on them. It doesn't know your seeding rate, soil or nitrogen. It runs in Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa, Illinois and Michigan, from September through June. Maps assume planting on October 1; at a location, enter your own planting date.[^agweather-rye-biomass]
