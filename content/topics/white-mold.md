White mold, also called Sclerotinia stem rot, is a disease of soybean caused by the fungus _Sclerotinia sclerotiorum_. It is a major soybean disease in the northern United States and Canada, and it is worst in cool, wet summers, in high-yielding fields with dense canopies. The same fungus attacks most broadleaf crops, including potato, snap bean, carrot, pea and the cabbage family, as well as many broadleaf weeds.[^smith-2026][^watson-smith-2026]

## Identification

- **On stems:** the first sign is a water-soaked patch at a node. It grows up and down the stem until it girdles it, and the stem dies, bleached and shredded.[^smith-2026]
- **Fungal growth:** in humid weather, white cottony growth covers the lesions. Hard black bodies about the size of mouse droppings, called sclerotia, form on and inside the stems and pods; split a stem to find them.[^watson-smith-2026][^smith-2026]
- **Whole plants:** leaves on infected stems wilt suddenly and die, and plants wilt, lodge and die, often in patches.[^watson-smith-2026][^smith-2026]
- **Look-alikes:** white mold can be confused with Phytophthora root rot, so look closely at affected plants.[^a3646-2025]

## Life cycle

The fungus survives in the soil as sclerotia for five years or more, and some survive at least eight.[^watson-smith-2026][^smith-2026] Sclerotia in the top 2 inches of soil sprout into small, mushroom-like cups called apothecia when the soil is shaded, moist and cool, which usually means after the crop canopy has closed. The cups release spores (ascospores) that infect open or dying flowers, and the fungus grows from the flower into the stem.[^smith-2026][^watson-smith-2026]

Apothecia are favored when the 30-day average of the daily high temperature is below 68 °F (20 °C), although in irrigated fields they can form even when it is above 80 °F (27 °C). Infection is favored by daily highs below 85 °F and frequent moisture. The risk is highest during flowering, from beginning bloom (R1) to beginning pod (R3), especially under a dense, nearly closed canopy.[^smith-2026] UW–Madison's guide puts it in Wisconsin terms: the risk rises when the 30-day average temperature is 70 °F or below and the weather is wet in the 30 days before bloom, and rises further if it stays wet or humid during bloom.[^a3646-2025]

Practices that raise yield also raise the risk: narrow rows, early planting, more than 160,000 plants per acre, irrigation and high soil fertility.[^a3646-2025]

## Damage

White mold reduces yield through fewer and lighter seeds. Sclerotia mixed in harvested grain can bring price discounts at the elevator, and infected seed may germinate poorly.[^smith-2026] Across the United States, white mold reduces the soybean crop by up to 1 million metric tons a year.[^willbur-2018]

## Monitoring

- Keep records of white mold in each field and variety. A field's history, the variety's resistance and the weather before and during bloom decide whether a fungicide is likely to pay.[^smith-2026][^a3646-2025]
- From beginning bloom, watch the canopy and the weather. A nearly closed canopy and cool, wet weather together raise the risk.[^smith-2026]
- Late in the season, look for patches of wilted or dead plants with bleached stems, and split stems to look for sclerotia. Note where the disease was, to guide next year's variety, row width and rotation.[^smith-2026]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls** reduce the number of sclerotia and make the canopy less favorable:

- **Plant resistant varieties.** No soybean variety is fully resistant, but partially resistant varieties are the most economical long-term control. UW–Madison's Wisconsin Soybean Variety Tests (A3654) rate varieties' reactions.[^watson-smith-2026][^a3646-2025]
- **Rotate.** Two to three years of corn or small grains reduce, but don't eliminate, the risk. Edible beans, snap beans, sunflowers, canola, cole crops and potatoes are hosts and keep sclerotia in the field. Control broadleaf weeds, which are hosts too.[^smith-2026][^a3646-2025]
- **Open the canopy in high-risk fields.** Moving from 15-inch to 30-inch rows can cut white mold by up to about half, though yield potential may drop. For fields at high risk of white mold, UW–Madison's guide suggests 100,000 to 140,000 seeds per acre in 15- or 30-inch rows.[^smith-2026][^a3646-2025]
- **Irrigate less often before and during flowering:** infrequent, heavy watering is better than frequent, light watering.[^smith-2026]
- **Keep sclerotia out:** plant clean seed, and harvest infested fields last so the combine doesn't carry sclerotia to other fields.[^watson-smith-2026]
- **Biological control:** a soil fungus, _Coniothyrium minitans_ (FRAC group BM02), attacks sclerotia.[^frac-2026] It is applied to the soil at least three months before white mold is likely and worked in about 2 inches deep. It can reduce sclerotia greatly but won't clear a heavy load.[^smith-2026][^a3646-2025]

**Fungicides** reduce white mold but never control it completely: in university trials they cut the disease by anywhere from none to about 60 %. Apply them from beginning bloom (R1) to beginning pod (R3), while flowers are present on the lower half of the stem. Sprays at R4 or later, or after symptoms appear, don't work.[^smith-2026][^watson-smith-2026][^a3646-2025]

- In UW–Madison's ratings, boscalid (an SDHI, FRAC group 7) and fluazinam (group 29) rate highest for white mold. Picoxystrobin (a QoI, group 11) and prothioconazole (a DMI, group 3) rate lower, and tetraconazole (group 3) and thiophanate-methyl (group 1) lower still. Some products are rated only for suppressing white mold, and some need two applications.[^a3646-2025]
- Thick canopies are hard to cover: use flat-fan nozzles that make droplets of 200 to 400 microns, and more water in dense canopies.[^smith-2026]
- The herbicide lactofen (WSSA group 14), applied at or before first bloom, suppresses white mold. It can injure the crop, especially in hot, dry weather.[^a3646-2025][^smith-2026][^wssa-hrac][^take-action-herbicides]

For products and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf). UW–Madison's [Sclerotinia stem rot fact sheet](https://pddc.wisc.edu/2015/08/16/sclerotinia-stem-rot-of-soybean/) and the Crop Protection Network's [An Overview of White Mold](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/publications/an-overview-of-white-mold) explain the choices in more detail. White mold of potato and vegetables is caused by the same fungus; for potato, where sprays start at full bloom or row closure, see UW–Madison Vegetable Pathology's [potato white mold page](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/diseases/potato-white-mold/).[^uw-vegpath-potato-white-mold]

## AgWeather's models

The [white mold model](/models/white-mold) estimates the probability that apothecia are present in a soybean field. It uses the equations of Willbur and others (2018), developed by a team including UW–Madison from whether apothecia were found in fields in Iowa, Michigan and Wisconsin: 30-day averages of the daily high temperature, high humidity and high wind speed for non-irrigated fields, and of temperature and humidity with row spacing for irrigated fields. In the paper's tests on new data, the equations were right about 67 to 70 % of the time.[^willbur-2018] UW–Madison's Sporecaster app used the same kinds of models, for non-irrigated fields and for irrigated fields in 15- or 30-inch rows; it has since been retired, and the Crop Protection Network now points growers to its Crop Risk Tool for white mold forecasts.[^smith-2018][^cpn-sporecaster]

We follow the 2025 version of the equations, which corrects one coefficient the Crop Risk Tool had copied wrongly, so for non-irrigated fields our probabilities run about one and a half times the tool's in summer. The model is inactive, and shows 0 %, while the 30-day average daily high is 50 °F (10 °C) or lower. We haven't compared it with white mold in Wisconsin fields. Its risk levels are the tool's: for non-irrigated fields, High starts at 35 %, a little below the 40 % at which Sporecaster advised a fungicide.[^agweather-white-mold][^tenenbaum-2018] Sporecaster also asked whether the canopy had closed; our model doesn't. The model predicts apothecia, not disease, and shows its risk only from R1 to R3, taken from the [soybean stages model](/models/soybean-stages) or your own dates.[^agweather-white-mold]
