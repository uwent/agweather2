Fusarium head blight, also called scab, is a fungal disease of wheat and barley, and of oats, rye and many grasses. It is caused mainly by _Fusarium graminearum_, the same fungus that causes [Gibberella ear rot](/topics/gibberella-ear-rot) and stalk rot of corn.[^friskop-2021][^smith-2026] When the weather favors it, it can cut yields by more than half, but the larger cost is usually grain quality: infected kernels carry the toxin deoxynivalenol (DON, or vomitoxin), and buyers discount or reject grain that has too much of it.[^friskop-2021]

## Identification

- **On the head:** spikelets bleach and ripen early while the rest of the head stays green. The bleaching can spread until the whole head dies, and the stem just below the head may turn brown.[^friskop-2021][^smith-2026][^a3646-2025]
- **Spores:** in wet weather, salmon-pink to orange spore masses form at the base of infected spikelets.[^friskop-2021][^a3646-2025]
- **Kernels:** scabby kernels are shrunken, light and chalky, white to pink or light brown, with a rough surface. Many blow out the back of the combine with the chaff. Late infections can leave kernels that look normal but still carry DON.[^friskop-2021][^smith-2026][^a3646-2025]
- **Look-alikes:** white heads can also come from root and foot rots, wheat stem maggot (which whitens heads before flowering), frost, drought and herbicide injury; these often show patterns such as field edges or sprayer strips.[^friskop-2021]

## Life cycle

The fungus survives the winter in crop residue, mostly corn stalks and the stubble of wheat, barley and other small grains, and in grasses. It lives longer on corn residue than on small-grain residue.[^smith-2026][^friskop-2021] Spores formed on the residue are carried by wind and rain to the flowering heads.[^friskop-2021]

Wheat is infected mainly at flowering. The spores need long periods of wet or humid weather, with relative humidity above 90 % and temperatures of about 59 to 86 °F; infection usually takes 48 to 72 hours of such weather.[^smith-2026][^friskop-2021] Warm, wet weather in the days before flowering builds up spores, and rain well before flowering does little. Infections can continue to the early dough stage if the weather stays wet, and fields whose plants are at mixed stages are at greater risk.[^kriss-2016][^smith-2026] Wet, humid weather between flowering and harvest can raise DON further, even when the risk of scab itself was low.[^kriss-2016]

Wheat planted after corn generally has more scab, because the residue carries the fungus.[^a3646-2025]

## Damage

- **Yield:** infected kernels are shrunken and light, lowering yield and test weight.[^friskop-2021]
- **DON:** grain with more than about 1 to 2 parts per million may be discounted or rejected. Don't feed wheat or barley with more than 10 % scabby kernels to pigs.[^friskop-2021][^a3646-2025]
- **Seed:** seed from scabby fields can rot or give seedling blight and root rot, thinning the stand. If you plant seed known to be infected, a fungicide seed treatment is recommended.[^a3646-2025][^friskop-2021]

## Monitoring

- Know when each field flowers. Check heads in several places across the field, since heads emerge unevenly.[^kriss-2016][^smith-2022]
- Check a risk forecast in the days before flowering. The national [Fusarium head blight risk tool](https://www.wheatscab.psu.edu/) estimates the risk of a scab epidemic for wheat at flowering from the humidity of the previous 15 days, for winter or spring wheat and your variety's resistance. It doesn't predict DON.[^kriss-2016][^harbach-2018][^fhb-risk-tool]
- Before harvest, look for bleached heads, and have grain from fields with scab tested for DON before you feed or sell it.[^friskop-2021]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Resistant varieties** are the most important practice. No variety is immune, but moderately resistant ones are available; check university variety trials for their ratings.[^friskop-2021][^a3646-2025]
- **Rotation:** don't plant wheat into small-grain or corn residue. Following soybean or another legume is best, with two to three years between small-grain crops.[^smith-2026][^friskop-2021]
- **Residue:** where it suits the field, plow under infested residue, and don't spread manure containing infested straw or cornstalks on small-grain fields.[^smith-2026]
- **Spread flowering:** growing several varieties that flower at different times keeps the whole crop from being exposed at once.[^smith-2026]
- **At harvest:** adjust the combine, including its fan speed, to blow out light, scabby kernels, keep poor grain separate, and dry and cool grain promptly for storage.[^friskop-2021]

**Fungicides** reduce scab and DON but don't control it fully on susceptible varieties in a bad year; together with a resistant variety they work best.[^friskop-2021][^kriss-2016]

- **Triazoles** (demethylation inhibitors, FRAC group 3) such as prothioconazole and tebuconazole, alone or mixed, have given the best control in university trials. Succinate dehydrogenase inhibitors (FRAC group 7) have done as well as some of the best triazoles in university trials across several states.[^smith-2026][^friskop-2021]
- **Timing:** spray the heads from the start of flowering (Feekes 10.5.1, Zadoks 61) to about 7 days after. In Wisconsin research, spraying about 5 days after flowering began gave the largest cut in DON, perhaps because more heads were in flower by then.[^friskop-2021][^smith-2022]
- **Avoid strobilurins** (quinone outside inhibitors, FRAC group 11) from boot onward: they can raise DON in the grain.[^friskop-2021][^smith-2022]

For products, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf). UW–Madison's [Fusarium head blight fact sheet](https://pddc.wisc.edu/2015/07/23/fusarium-head-blight/) and the Crop Protection Network's [overview of Fusarium head blight](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/publications/an-overview-of-fusarium-head-blight) explain the disease and its management in more detail.

## AgWeather's models

The [wheat scab model](/models/wheat-scab) gives the probability of a Fusarium head blight epidemic from the average relative humidity of the previous 14 days, for each of four classes of variety resistance (very susceptible, susceptible, moderately susceptible and moderately resistant; susceptible unless you choose another). Risk is Low from 1 %, Moderate from 24 % and High from 32 %.[^agweather-wheat-scab]

The model was copied from the Crop Protection Network's Crop Risk Tool and matches it exactly on the same weather. The tool credits its equation to Erick De Wolf and, on another page, to equations by Shah, Paul, De Wolf and Madden; we haven't yet found the published paper it comes from. It hasn't been compared with scab in Wisconsin fields.[^agweather-wheat-scab]

The probability means something only for wheat flowering on the day shown. The model doesn't yet know when your field flowers, so it shows a value every day of the year, including winter days with no crop to infect, when it often reads Moderate or High. Ordinary humidity gives high values, too: a two-week average of 70 % puts a susceptible variety at about 30 % (Moderate), and 80 % at about 41 % (High).[^agweather-wheat-scab] Read it only in the days around your field's flowering, and compare it with the national risk tool, which asks for the date your wheat flowers.[^kriss-2016]
