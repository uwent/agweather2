The alfalfa weevil (_Hypera postica_) is a small snout beetle and one of the main insect pests of alfalfa in Wisconsin. Its larvae feed on the growing tips of the first crop in spring, and in heavy years they can strip plants, cut yield and lower forage quality.[^bick-2025][^a3646-2025] Timely cutting controls it in most fields, so scouting is about deciding whether a field can wait for harvest.

## Identification

- **Larvae** have no legs and a shiny black head. Small larvae are slate-colored; grown larvae are bright green with a white stripe down the back and reach about ⅜ inch.[^bick-2025][^hanson-potter-2023][^a3646-2025]
- **Adults** are gray-brown snout beetles about ¼ inch long, with a darker stripe or shield-shaped mark down the back.[^bick-2025][^a3646-2025][^cpn-2023]
- **Eggs** are white to yellow and laid inside alfalfa stems.[^bick-2025][^cpn-2023]
- The clover leaf weevil looks similar and is easily confused with it.[^cpn-2023]

## Life cycle

Adults spend the winter in leaf litter near alfalfa fields. In spring they move into the fields and lay eggs in the stems. Some eggs are laid the autumn before, but most are laid in spring.[^bick-2025][^a3646-2025] The larvae pass through four stages (instars) as they feed, then spin silken cocoons on the plants or in litter on the ground and become adults 1 to 2 weeks later.[^bick-2025][^a3646-2025] These summer adults feed for a short time, then leave the field and rest until autumn. There is one generation a year.[^a3646-2025][^hanson-potter-2023]

Weevils develop faster in warm weather, so [degree days](/topics/degree-days) predict their stages better than the calendar. UW–Madison counts them from a base of 48 °F: egg hatch and tip feeding begin at about 300 degree days, and the heaviest feeding comes between about 600 and 800.[^bick-2025][^jensen-2018] In most years the peak of larval feeding falls between mid-May and early June and drops off by mid-June. Southern Wisconsin usually reaches each stage first, and central and northern Wisconsin later.[^a3646-2025][^jensen-2018]

Minnesota reports a western strain of the weevil that develops 1 to 2 weeks later than the eastern strain on which degree-day models are based. Models predict the first feeding better than the end of the season.[^hanson-potter-2023]

## Damage

- **Tip feeding:** young larvae chew small holes in the leaves at the stem tips. Older larvae skeletonize the leaves, and a heavily infested field takes on a grayish cast.[^bick-2025][^a3646-2025][^cpn-2023]
- **Yield and quality:** larval feeding on the first crop does most of the damage. It lowers yield and the feeding value of the forage, weakens the plants and can thin the stand.[^bick-2025][^cpn-2023][^a3646-2025]
- **Regrowth:** larvae that survive cutting, and new adults, can feed on the second crop. In bad years, the weevils eat the new shoots on the crowns as soon as they appear, and the field doesn't green up after cutting.[^a3646-2025]

## Monitoring

- Start spot-checking at about 300 degree days (base 48 °F). Look first on warm, south-facing slopes and sandy knolls, where feeding usually shows first.[^bick-2025]
- Pick 50 stems at random across the field and count those with tip feeding. A sweep net shows whether larvae are present, but don't base a decision on sweep counts.[^bick-2025]
- **First crop:** treat when 40 % of stems show feeding and the field is more than a week from harvest. Forty percent means 40 % of stems with signs of feeding, not 40 % of the leaves eaten. If the field is within 7 to 10 days of harvest, cut it early instead.[^bick-2025][^jensen-2018][^a3646-2025]
- **After cutting:** check the stubble 4 to 5 days after harvest. Treat if larvae are still active and 50 % of stems show feeding, or if larvae or adults are present and there is no sign of regrowth 3 to 4 days after harvest.[^bick-2025][^a3646-2025]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Timely cutting** is often the best control. Cutting with a mower conditioner kills many larvae. Some survive under the windrows and feed on the regrowth, so check the stubble.[^bick-2025][^hanson-potter-2023]
- **Natural enemies**, including parasitic wasps and predatory beetles, help keep weevil numbers down. Insecticides kill them too, so use insecticides only when a field passes the threshold.[^bick-2025]

**Insecticides** are applied to the standing crop when tip feeding passes the threshold. Groups used against the weevil include carbamates (IRAC group 1A; carbaryl, methomyl), organophosphates (group 1B; malathion, phosmet), pyrethroids (group 3A) and indoxacarb (group 22). Spinosad (group 5) is an organic option but gives only partial control. Carbaryl can burn regrowth if applied after cutting, and group 1B products work better in mixtures than alone.[^bick-2025][^hanson-potter-2023] Weevils have become resistant to insecticides where the same ones are used often. Pyrethroid resistance has been confirmed in western states, and reports of control failures in Minnesota have raised concern there. Rotate groups, and if a treatment seems to fail, don't follow it with the same group.[^bick-2025][^hanson-potter-2023]

For products and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf). UW–Madison's [Managing Alfalfa Weevil in Wisconsin Alfalfa Fields](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/articles/managing-alfalfa-weevil-in-wisconsin-alfalfa-fields) and the University of Minnesota's [alfalfa weevil recommendations](https://blog-crop-news.extension.umn.edu/2023/05/updated-alfalfa-weevil-management.html) explain scouting and treatment in more detail.

## AgWeather's models

The [alfalfa weevil model](/models/alfalfa-weevil) counts degree days from January 1 (single sine, base 48.2 °F, or 9 °C, with an upper limit of 86 °F) and marks the weevil's stages: egg hatch and the start of scouting at 300, the second, third and fourth larval stages at 370, 440 and 505, pupae at 595, new adults at 815 and their summer rest at 1,000. Risk is highest from 440 to 595, while the larger larvae feed.[^agweather-alfalfa-weevil]

The model was copied from the Crop Protection Network's Crop Risk Tool, and it matches the tool day for day. The tool cites no study, but its larval stages after egg hatch fall within 3 degree days of the development times measured in Wisconsin by Litsinger and Apple in 1973, and its adult emergence within 2 of Harcourt's 1981 model from Ontario. We found no study behind egg hatch at 300 degree days from January 1, and the model hasn't been compared with weevil counts or tip feeding in Wisconsin fields.[^agweather-alfalfa-weevil] It uses one base for every stage, while the Wisconsin study found eggs develop above 50 °F, and it can't account for eggs laid the autumn before.[^agweather-alfalfa-weevil]

Its timing also differs from UW–Madison's advice. UW–Madison puts the heaviest feeding at about 600 to 800 degree days, while the model's risk peaks earlier and it marks the end of scouting at 595, when pupation begins.[^jensen-2018][^agweather-alfalfa-weevil] Keep checking fields after that if larvae are still feeding, and check the stubble after the first cutting.
