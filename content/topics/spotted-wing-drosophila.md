Spotted wing drosophila (_Drosophila suzukii_) is a small, invasive fruit fly. Most vinegar flies lay their eggs only in damaged or overripe fruit, but this one cuts into sound, ripening fruit, so its larvae spoil fruit before it can be picked. It attacks raspberry, blackberry, strawberry, blueberry, cherry and other soft fruit, and it also breeds in more than 40 wild plants, such as buckthorn, honeysuckle, dogwood and mulberry.[^uw-a4133] It was first reported in Wisconsin in 2010, and breeding populations and infested fruit followed in 2012.[^uw-hort-swd]

## Identification

- **Adults** are 1/16 to ⅛ inch long, with red eyes and a banded abdomen. Look at them with a hand lens of at least 10×.[^uw-a4133]
- **Males** have a single dark spot near the tip of each wing.[^uw-a4133]
- **Females** have no wing spots. Their egg-laying organ, the ovipositor, carries two rows of dark, saw-like teeth, best seen at 30× or more.[^uw-a4133]
- **Larvae** are cream-colored, legless maggots up to ⅛ inch long, found inside the fruit. Eggs and larvae look like those of other fruit flies.[^uw-hort-swd][^uw-a4133]

## Life cycle

In Wisconsin, adults are thought to spend the winter in a hardier winter form.[^uw-a4133] Some survive Minnesota winters, but most of the flies there each year are thought to arrive from farther south.[^umn-swd] In Wisconsin, traps first catch adults from late June to early July. The flies can build up in wild berries in woodlands and then spread into fruit plantings in midsummer.[^uw-a4133]

A female lays one to three eggs in each fruit and up to several hundred in her life. The eggs hatch in 12 to 72 hours, and the larvae feed inside the fruit for 5 to 7 days. Most then drop to the ground to pupate in the leaf litter.[^uw-a4133] At 68 to 77 °F, a generation takes only 8 to 12 days, so numbers can rise from the first catch to damaging levels in a short time.[^uw-a4133] Populations keep building through summer and early fall, which puts fall-bearing crops at the highest risk.[^uw-hort-swd]

## Damage

The first sign is a soft, slightly discolored spot on the fruit. As the larvae feed, the fruit wrinkles and collapses, and the hole the female cut lets rot organisms in. Buyers reject fruit with larvae in it. Left uncontrolled, the fly can infest all the fruit in a planting.[^uw-a4133] Summer-bearing raspberries usually finish before the fly peaks, while fall raspberries, blackberries and other late crops ripen as numbers climb.[^uw-a4133][^uw-hort-swd]

## Monitoring

- **Traps:** hang one trap per acre in the shaded canopy as soon as green fruit appears. Bait homemade traps with a mix of yeast, sugar and water, or use a commercial trap and lure, and check traps once or twice a week. Not everything in the trap will be spotted wing drosophila, so check the flies under magnification.[^uw-a4133][^uw-fruit-threshold]
- **When to act:** once fruit is ripening and traps catch at least one fly per trap in a week, start control.[^uw-fruit-threshold]
- **Fruit checks:** traps show that flies are present; checking fruit shows the damage. Put a sample of fruit in a sealable bag, crush it lightly, and add ¼ cup of salt in 4 cups of water. Larvae leave the fruit and float.[^uw-a4133]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Pick often.** In a two-year raspberry study reported by UW–Madison's fruit program, fruit picked every one or two days held fewer eggs and larvae than fruit picked every three days. Picking every day still left some, so combine frequent picking with other controls.[^uw-fruit-harvest]
- **Sanitation:** remove damaged, overripe and fallen fruit. Don't compost infested fruit: bury or freeze it, or seal it in plastic bags. In research the program reports, 32 hours in a sealed plastic bag left in a sunny field killed 99 % of the larvae.[^uw-a4133][^uw-fruit-harvest]
- **Cool fruit quickly** after harvest to slow any eggs and larvae inside.[^uw-a4133]
- **Open the canopy:** the fly prefers shade and humidity, so prune well and use drip rather than overhead irrigation.[^uw-a4133]
- **Netting** with a mesh of 1/32 inch or finer keeps flies off small plantings. Put it on after fruit set, so it doesn't block pollination, and before the fruit ripens, and seal the edges.[^uw-a4133] The University of Minnesota reports good results with 80-gram insect netting.[^umn-swd]
- **Choose crops and varieties that ripen early,** such as summer-bearing raspberries, to harvest before numbers peak.[^uw-a4133]

**Insecticides** work only partly, and best together with frequent picking and sanitation. Carbamates (IRAC group 1A), organophosphates (group 1B), pyrethroids (group 3A) and spinosyns (group 5) have given good control in conventional production, and neonicotinoids (group 4A) weaker control; spinosad and pyrethrum (group 3A) are allowed in organic production.[^uw-a4133][^irac-moa] UW–Madison rates pyrethroids, diamides (group 28), spinosyns and the organophosphate malathion as excellent, and in organic production suggests rotating spinosad with pyrethrum.[^uw-fruit-threshold] Start when the fruit begins to color and flies are being caught, and keep fruit covered until harvest is over, respraying every 4 to 5 days depending on the product and keeping to its preharvest interval.[^uw-a4133][^umn-swd] Spray thoroughly, into the shaded center of the plant where the flies rest, and rotate insecticide classes to slow resistance. To protect pollinators, don't spray during bloom or while bees are flying.[^uw-a4133]

For products and current registrations, see the [Midwest Fruit Pest Management Guide](https://edustore.purdue.edu/id-465-w.html). See also UW–Madison's [spotted wing drosophila publication](https://fruit.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/36/2022/01/A4133-Spotted-Wing-Drosophila.pdf) and the University of Minnesota's [spotted wing drosophila page](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/yard-and-garden-insects/spotted-wing-drosophila); for home gardens, see UW–Madison's [spotted wing drosophila article](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/spotted-wing-drosophila/).

## AgWeather's models

The [spotted wing drosophila model](/models/spotted-wing-drosophila) estimates how active adult flies are through the season, as a share of the usual peak, from [degree days](/topics/degree-days): a running total of warmth between 45 and 86 °F (7.2 and 30 °C, the range in which the fly develops), counted from January 1. Its curve is fitted to the weekly trap catches of a UW–Madison study in Door County tart cherry orchards from 2015 to 2018, refitted on AgWeather's weather.[^kamiyama-2020][^agweather-swd]

- **First flies:** traps in the study first caught flies at about 800 to 1,100 degree days, and the model's activity reaches Low at about 1,130. In AgWeather's averages for 1996–2025, 1,130 degree days comes around June 23 at Madison and July 11 at Sturgeon Bay.
- **Peak:** numbers climb through July and August to a peak at about 2,330 degree days, around August 10 at Madison and August 29 at Sturgeon Bay. The study found its peak at the same point once its degree days are converted to AgWeather's.
- **After the peak:** activity falls slowly in the model, but only one season of the study ran into October. Where the season runs longer, as in southern Wisconsin, flies may stay numerous into fall.

The study's published numbers (first flies at 1,276 degree days and a peak at 3,180) were computed with a lower base temperature than the 45 °F it reports, so they run about one and a half times AgWeather's degree days. The earlier version of this model used them as they were, which put its peak about a month after the flies' in Door County.[^agweather-swd]

The model describes a typical season in sprayed cherry orchards, not a particular planting, and traps in the same orchard caught their first flies weeks apart. Don't wait for the model: hang traps when green fruit appears, and act on what they catch.
