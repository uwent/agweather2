The onion maggot (_Delia antiqua_) is a small fly whose maggots feed below ground on onions and their relatives: leeks, shallots, garlic and chives. It feeds on nothing else. It is often the most serious insect pest of onions, especially where onions are grown on the same ground year after year, and problems tend to build after a run of cool, wet springs.[^uw-vegento-om][^a3422-2026][^salgado-2025]

## Identification

- **Adults** are gray flies about ¼ to ⅓ inch long that look like small houseflies, with the wings overlapping over the body at rest. They are larger than seedcorn maggot flies.[^schuh-2022][^uw-vegento-om][^salgado-2025]
- **Eggs** are white and elongated, laid at the base of the plant and in nearby soil cracks.[^uw-vegento-om][^schuh-2022]
- **Larvae** are cream-colored, legless maggots about ¼ inch long when grown, tapering to a point at the head. They tunnel in roots and bulbs.[^uw-vegento-om][^a3422-2026][^schuh-2022]

Seedcorn maggots also attack onions. The larvae of the two species are told apart by counting the tiny lobes on the breathing openings near the head.[^salgado-2025]

## Life cycle

Onion maggots spend the winter as pupae in the soil, including in cull onions and cull piles. In Wisconsin, adults begin to emerge in mid-May. Females lay eggs a few days after emerging, and the larvae crawl down under the leaf sheaths into the bulb. They feed for 2 to 3 weeks, passing through three stages, then pupate in the soil, and the next generation of flies appears 3 to 4 weeks later.[^uw-vegento-om][^schuh-2022]

There are three generations a year in Wisconsin. UW–Madison's scouting calendar has flies active from mid-May to mid-June, from early July to mid-August, and from late August through September.[^uw-vegento-om] Trapping in New York and Ontario found the same three flights, and in New York the most flies were caught in the spring flight.[^eckenrode-1975][^liu-1982]

The flies develop faster in warm weather, so [degree days](/topics/degree-days) predict their flights better than the calendar. UW–Madison uses a base of 40 °F, counted from when the ground thaws, and puts the three flight peaks at about 680, 1,950 and 3,230 degree days.[^uw-vegento-om] Local conditions matter: in New York the spring flight's midpoint varied from about 500 to 960 degree days between sites and years.[^eckenrode-1975]

## Damage

Seedlings are most at risk. Maggots feed in the stem below ground, and plants wilt, yellow and collapse; many die, leaving gaps and thin stands. Uncontrolled, heavy infestations can destroy a whole stand.[^uw-vegento-om][^salgado-2025]

Later generations tunnel into larger bulbs. Bulbs damaged late in the season are unmarketable and can rot in storage, since the tunnels let disease organisms in.[^uw-vegento-om][^eckenrode-1975] UW–Madison finds onion sets the most susceptible, followed by white, yellow and red onions.[^uw-vegento-om]

## Monitoring

- By the time damage shows, it is too late to control the maggots, so decisions rest on knowing when flies are active.[^uw-vegento-om][^schuh-2022]
- Follow degree days or UW–Madison's scouting calendar to know when each flight is due.[^uw-vegento-om]
- Researchers follow flights with cone or sticky traps at field edges, often baited with cull onions. Catches depend heavily on the weather, so the date when half a flight has been caught is a steadier guide than a single peak.[^eckenrode-1975][^liu-1982]
- Keep a record of stand loss each year. Damage of more than 5 to 10 % in last year's crop is UW–Madison's signal to use a preventive insecticide at planting.[^uw-vegento-om]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Rotation and distance:** plant new onion fields at least a mile from last year's onions, and keep fields at least ¼ mile apart, so flies emerging from old fields don't reach new ones. Small farms may not have room for this.[^uw-vegento-om][^salgado-2025]
- **Sanitation:** remove cull onions from the field after harvest, and destroy crop debris and cull piles, or bury culls deep within a pile. Volunteer onions also carry maggots into the next season.[^uw-vegento-om][^a3422-2026][^schuh-2022][^salgado-2025]
- **Organic matter:** avoid working in green manure before planting, since decaying plant material attracts egg-laying flies.[^uw-vegento-om]
- **Planting time:** delaying planting can let young onions miss the first flight, though it costs bulb size. UW–Madison notes that onion sets planted a week before flies are expected to emerge are damaged less. Transplants withstand attack better than seeded onions.[^salgado-2025][^schuh-2022][^uw-vegento-om]
- **Row covers:** in gardens, cover onions by early to mid-May and keep the cover on until egg-laying ends. Don't use covers where onions grew the year before, since flies can emerge underneath.[^schuh-2022]

**Insecticides** work only as prevention at planting: on the seed, as a drench for transplant trays or applied in the furrow. Seed treatments include spinosad (IRAC group 5), cyromazine (group 17) and neonicotinoids (group 4A); rotate between groups from year to year.[^salgado-2025][^a3422-2026] Sprays against adult flies are usually not successful, because the flies move between fields and avoid exposure.[^uw-vegento-om][^a3422-2026][^salgado-2025] Onion maggots have become resistant to some insecticides, including the organophosphate diazinon in other Midwest states.[^uw-vegento-om][^a3422-2026] Home gardeners have no insecticides for use at planting and rely on the nonchemical controls above.[^schuh-2022]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [onion maggot page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/onion-maggot/) and Cornell's [onion and seedcorn maggot fact sheet](https://cals.cornell.edu/integrated-pest-management/outreach-education/fact-sheets/onion-maggot-and-seedcorn-maggot-delia-antiqua-and-delia-platura) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see the University of Minnesota's [cabbage and onion maggot page](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/yard-and-garden-insects/root-maggots).

## AgWeather's models

The [onion maggot model](/models/onion-maggot) rates the risk of fly activity from 0 to 4 for two flights, peaking at 680 and 1,950 degree days from January 1 (base 39.2 °F, upper limit 86 °F), the timing of the first two of UW–Madison's three peaks. It doesn't yet include the third flight, in late August and September, which UW's calendar and New York trapping both show.[^agweather-om][^uw-vegento-om][^eckenrode-1975] The model was carried over unchanged from the Vegetable Disease and Insect Forecasting Network (VDIFN), and given the same degree days it gives the same risk levels. The source of its numbers hasn't been identified, and its timing hasn't been checked against published research, Wisconsin trap catches or NEWA's onion maggot model.[^agweather-om]
