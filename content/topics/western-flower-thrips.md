The western flower thrips (_Frankliniella occidentalis_) is a tiny insect that feeds on the flowers, leaves and fruit of a wide range of plants. It is perhaps the most serious pest of greenhouse crops in the world, and among vegetables it attacks tomato, pepper and cucurbits.[^uw-hort][^umn][^ucipm][^ucipm-pn] Its greatest harm is often the viruses it spreads, above all tomato spotted wilt virus.[^ucipm][^umass] It does not survive the winter outdoors in the Upper Midwest; it breeds all year in greenhouses and travels on young plants.[^umn][^uw-hort] In Wisconsin onion and cabbage fields, the thrips that matter most is a different species, the onion thrips (_Thrips tabaci_).[^uw-vegento][^a3422-2026]

## Identification

- **Adults** are slender and less than 1/16 inch long, with two pairs of narrow wings fringed with long hairs. Males are light yellow; females are larger and range from light yellow to dark brown.[^umn][^umass]
- **Larvae** look like small, pale, wingless adults.[^ucipm]
- **Telling it from onion thrips** takes a hand lens and practice: western flower thrips have red simple eyes (ocelli) on top of the head, where onion thrips' are gray, and are slightly larger.[^ucipm]

## Life cycle

Females lay 130 to 300 eggs each, cut into leaves, flower parts and petals, where sprays rarely reach them. The larvae feed in buds, flowers and young leaves through two stages, then drop to the soil or growing medium, where they pass through two nonfeeding stages, the prepupa and pupa, before becoming adults.[^umn][^uw-hort][^umass] At greenhouse temperatures of 62 to 98 °F, a generation takes as little as 7 to 13 days, so numbers build quickly and generations overlap. Development stops below about 50 °F, though the insects survive cooler weather.[^umn][^uw-hort]

In heated greenhouses, western flower thrips breed all year.[^umn] They do survive winters outdoors farther south, in the soil and in clover in the Mid-Atlantic states, but not in the Upper Midwest.[^umn][^umass] They spread on plugs, cuttings and young plants, and adults, though weak fliers, drift on air currents and on clothing.[^uw-hort][^ucipm] Hot, dry weather favors severe thrips problems in Wisconsin vegetables.[^a3422-2026]

## Damage

- **Feeding:** thrips pierce plant cells and suck out their contents. The damage shows as silvery or whitish flecks and patches with tiny black specks of droppings, and as deformed flowers, leaves and shoots.[^uw-hort][^ucipm]
- **Virus:** western flower thrips spread tomato spotted wilt virus and impatiens necrotic spot virus, which infect hundreds of plant species. Only larvae can pick up the virus, from infected plants, but the adults they become can spread it for the rest of their lives. Infected plants can't be cured.[^ucipm][^umass][^uw-hort]

## Monitoring

- Inspect transplants and greenhouse plants for adults and larvae as soon as they arrive, and keep infested plants apart.[^uw-hort]
- Sticky cards show when adults are flying. Blue cards catch western flower thrips better than yellow ones; yellow cards also catch aphids and whiteflies.[^umass][^ucipm]
- In fields, set sticky traps along the edges to catch the first thrips moving in, and check plants on field edges weekly, where thrips are most common early in the season.[^uw-vegento]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Clean transplants:** start with thrips-free, virus-free plants, and buy from growers who control thrips.[^umass][^uw-hort]
- **Weeds and neighboring crops:** weeds host both the thrips and the viruses, so control them in and around greenhouses and fields. Where you can, keep peppers away from onions, garlic, small grains and greenhouses of ornamentals, where thrips build up.[^umass][^ucipm]
- **Reflective mulch** repels thrips from young plants early in the season.[^ucipm]
- **Natural enemies:** in greenhouses, predatory mites and the minute pirate bug (_Orius insidiosus_) are released to control thrips.[^umass]
- **Remove plants with spotted wilt** promptly.[^uw-hort]

**Insecticides** are hard to make work: thrips hide in buds and flowers, the eggs and pupae are protected, numbers build fast, and many populations resist several insecticides.[^uw-hort][^umn] Direct sprays at where thrips feed, with good coverage, and rotate classes: keep to one class for at least a generation, two to three weeks in a warm greenhouse, then switch to another.[^uw-hort][^umass] Spinosyns (IRAC group 5; spinosad) are among the more effective classes, though resistance to spinosad has been reported.[^ucipm-pn][^umass] In California, transplants of pepper are treated with a neonicotinoid (group 4A; imidacloprid) to slow the spread of spotted wilt.[^ucipm][^irac-moa] For onion thrips in Wisconsin onions, UW–Madison suggests treating at seven or more thrips per plant.[^a3422-2026][^uw-vegento]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [thrips page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/thrips/) covers onion thrips in Wisconsin fields, and UMass Extension's [western flower thrips fact sheet](https://www.umass.edu/agriculture-food-environment/greenhouse-floriculture/fact-sheets/western-flower-thrips-management-tospoviruses) covers greenhouses in more detail. For home greenhouses, see UW–Madison's [Managing Thrips in Greenhouses](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/managing-thrips-greenhouses).

## AgWeather's models

AgWeather has no western flower thrips model. An earlier one, carried over from VDIFN, counted degree days from January 1 at a base of 45 °F, following Oregon State's western flower thrips model, which is still listed as under development,[^uspest-wft] as if thrips had spent the winter in the field, which western flower thrips don't do in Wisconsin: they arrive on transplants or from greenhouses, on their own schedule.[^umn][^uw-hort] It was retired in 2026, and its validation report says why.[^agweather-western-flower-thrips] Scout transplants and field edges for the thrips themselves.
