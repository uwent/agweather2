Two beetles, the northern corn rootworm (_Diabrotica barberi_) and the western corn rootworm (_Diabrotica virgifera virgifera_), are the rootworms that damage field and sweet corn in Wisconsin. Their larvae feed on corn roots, so the damage comes mainly where corn follows corn, and is greatest in the southern two-thirds of the state on loam soils; rootworms seldom matter on muck or on unirrigated sands. A third species, the southern corn rootworm (the spotted cucumber beetle), arrives too late in summer to harm Wisconsin corn.[^uw-vegento]

## Identification

- **Larvae** are slender and white, about ½ inch long, with a brown to black head and a dark plate on top of the last segment. Northern and western larvae can't be told apart in the field.[^uw-vegento]
- **Beetles** are about ¼ inch long. Northern beetles are pale yellow to tan at first, turning light green; western beetles are yellow with black stripes along the wing covers, which run together on males.[^uw-vegento]

## Life cycle

Both species spend the winter as eggs in the top 6 inches of soil, laid the summer before, mostly in cornfields. The eggs develop once the soil warms to 50 to 52 °F in spring.[^uw-vegento][^a3646-2025] In central Iowa, peak hatch comes around June 6 in an average year, and hatch lasts about a month.[^iowa-crw]

Larvae are feeding on roots by mid-June and peak in early to mid-July; each feeds for about three weeks, then pupates in the soil.[^uw-vegento][^a3646-2025] Beetles appear in July at UW–Madison's Arlington research station, and because eggs hatch over a long period, larvae, pupae and beetles can all be found in July and August.[^a3646-2025][^uw-vegento] Beetles feed on corn pollen and silks, and western beetles also on leaves. Females lay 300 to 500 eggs each, most of them from mid to late August, and more in moist soil than in dry.[^uw-vegento][^a3646-2025]

Two variants defeat crop rotation. A strain of western corn rootworm lays eggs in soybean fields; it has been confirmed in Kenosha, Racine, Walworth and Rock counties, though reports have been infrequent in recent years. Some northern corn rootworm eggs wait through two winters before hatching (extended diapause), which is more common west of Wisconsin and hasn't been confirmed in the state.[^a3646-2025]

## Damage

- **Root feeding:** larvae scar, tunnel into and prune roots. Plants with pruned roots lodge after heavy rain and wind; slightly lodged plants curve back upward ("goosenecking") and carry lighter ears, and badly lodged ones may produce no ear. Storms can lodge corn without rootworms, so check the roots.[^uw-vegento]
- **Silk clipping:** beetles eating green silks can reduce pollination, mostly in late-planted field corn and sweet corn, since most field corn is pollinated before beetle numbers peak.[^uw-vegento]

## Monitoring

- **Roots:** in July or August, dig plants with a 6-inch core of soil, wash the roots and look for brown, tunneled or pruned roots; by fall, normal root decay hides the damage.[^uw-vegento] Iowa State rates injury from 0 to 3 nodes of roots pruned; a rating of 0.25 is the economic injury level.[^iowa-crw]
- **Silk clipping:** before 70 % of plants are silking, count beetles on 10 plants in each of five areas and note silks clipped to ¼ inch or less.[^uw-vegento] UW–Madison's field crops guide suggests a spray to protect pollination only when silks are being clipped, pollination is less than half done and there are five or more beetles per plant.[^a3646-2025]
- **Next year's risk:** from late July through late August, count beetles on the whole plant, ear tip included, on 50 plants three times, 7 to 10 days apart.[^uw-vegento] If counts average 0.75 or more beetles per plant in August, plan a rootworm control if the field goes back to corn.[^a3646-2025] Minnesota notes that these thresholds date from the 1980s and that none exist yet for Bt rootworm hybrids.[^umn-crw]
- **Soybean fields** in southeastern Wisconsin can be checked with unbaited yellow sticky traps for the soybean-laying variant.[^a3646-2025]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Crop rotation** is the most reliable control: larvae that hatch in a field not in corn starve. Corn after soybeans seldom needs a rootworm treatment unless the soybeans were weedy or full of volunteer corn, whose pollen draws egg-laying beetles; nor does corn after most vegetables, except lima beans, whose long bloom attracts beetles.[^a3646-2025][^uw-vegento]
- **Late-planted fields** draw beetles from older corn nearby and can end up with heavy egg numbers; rotating those fields is the better choice.[^a3646-2025]
- **Natural enemies**, such as ground beetles and predatory mites, eat some eggs, larvae and pupae.[^uw-vegento]

**Bt hybrids** with rootworm proteins (Cry3Bb1, mCry3A, eCry3.1Ab, Cry34/35Ab1) or an RNA interference trait protect roots, with a refuge required. Both species have evolved resistance since the first Bt rootworm corn was sold in 2003, so UW–Madison advises rotating crops, not using the same Bt protein more than two years running, and checking roots.[^a3646-2025] The University of Minnesota reports western corn rootworms confirmed resistant to all Bt rootworm traits.[^umn-crw]

**Insecticides:** soil insecticides applied in the furrow or in a band at planting include pyrethroids (IRAC group 3A, such as tefluthrin and bifenthrin) and organophosphates (group 1B, such as terbufos). Neonicotinoid seed treatments (group 4A, clothianidin or thiamethoxam) at the rootworm rate are an option where last year's beetle counts were low to moderate. A soil insecticide together with a Bt hybrid is worth it only after very high beetle counts the year before. Sprays against egg-laying beetles don't replace larval control, are rarely warranted in Wisconsin and are hazardous to bees. Rotate insecticide classes from year to year.[^a3646-2025]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf) for field corn and [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/) for sweet corn, or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [corn rootworm page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/corn-rootworm/) and Minnesota's [scouting page](https://extension.umn.edu/corn-pest-management/scouting-corn-rootworm) give more detail.

## AgWeather's models

The [corn rootworm model](/models/corn-rootworm) counts degree days in air temperature from January 1, base 52 °F with no upper limit. Its egg hatch comes from a study of northern corn rootworm eggs from Wisconsin fields: hatch begins at about 300 degree days and is half done at 533, the air total on the day eggs buried in a Madison field reached half hatched.[^apple-1971] Larvae feed on roots for about 550 degree days after hatching, the model's risk, as larvae took from hatch to pupation in New York cornfields, and the pupa takes about 200 more, as in the laboratory.[^davis-1996][^jackson-elliott-1988] Half the beetles are then out at about 1,283 degree days. It also marks a window to dig and rate roots.[^agweather-crw]

In AgWeather's long-term averages (1996–2025), half the eggs hatch around June 14 at Arlington and June 26 at Rhinelander, one to three weeks after Iowa State's average peak hatch in central Iowa (June 6), and larvae peak around July 3 at Arlington and July 17 at Rhinelander, in UW–Madison's early to mid-July.[^iowa-crw][^uw-vegento] The first beetles come around July 15 at Arlington, the day the long-term averages reach the 1,300 degree days above 50 °F at which UW–Madison's degree-day table has adult beetles present, and 10 % are out by July 18. An older three-year study at Arlington found the first beetles between July 16 and 24.[^uw-degree-days][^uw-vegento][^agweather-crw] The hatch timing is the northern species', and it serves the western species too: in laboratory studies with temperatures that change as they do in the field, half of western corn rootworm eggs hatched within the span of the model's hatch, while at constant temperatures they took longer.[^levine-1992][^wilde-1971][^agweather-crw] The larvae of the two species can't be told apart in the field. Eggs and larvae live in the soil, and the model counts air temperature. The Wisconsin study's eggs were in bare soil, and in Minnesota eggs under crop residue or weeds hatched later than eggs in bare soil, so in many fields every stage may come later than the model's dates.[^chiang-sisson-1968] The model hasn't been compared with field counts of larvae or beetles in Wisconsin, so check roots and count beetles in your own fields.
