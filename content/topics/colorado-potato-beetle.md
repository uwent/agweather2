The Colorado potato beetle (_Leptinotarsa decemlineata_) is a leaf beetle that feeds on potato, eggplant, pepper, tomato and related weeds such as nightshades, horse-nettle and buffalo-bur, though it prefers potato. Adults and larvae both eat the leaves, and left alone they can strip plants bare. It remains a major pest of commercial potatoes in Wisconsin.[^uw-vegento-cpb][^a3422-2026]

## Identification

- **Adults** are oval, domed beetles about ⅜ inch long. The yellow wing covers carry ten narrow black stripes, and the head and the area behind it are orange with black spots.[^uw-vegento-cpb][^schuh-2026]
- **Eggs** are bright yellow to orange, laid in clusters of about 10 to 30 on the undersides of leaves. A female can lay 300 to 800 eggs.[^uw-vegento-cpb]
- **Larvae** are soft and humpbacked, with black heads and legs and two rows of black spots along each side. Newly hatched larvae are dark and pinhead-sized; older ones turn brick red to orange-pink and reach about ⅜ inch.[^uw-vegento-cpb][^schuh-2026][^a3422-2026]

## Life cycle

Adults spend the winter in the soil, about 4 to 12 inches down, mostly at field edges, in grassy borders and near trees. They come out at about the time potatoes emerge, usually in May, and colonize the nearest host plants, usually less than a mile away, so the first beetles show up along field edges.[^uw-vegento-cpb][^a3422-2026][^schuh-2026]

Eggs hatch in 4 to 9 days, depending on temperature. Larvae pass through four stages (instars) over 2 to 3 weeks, then drop to the soil to pupate, and new adults come out 10 to 14 days later. South-central Wisconsin usually has two generations a year and northern Wisconsin one; first-generation adults, the summer adults, appear from about mid-July, and some lay eggs for a partial second generation before they leave for their winter sites as the crop dies back.[^uw-vegento-cpb]

Because the beetles develop faster in warm weather, [degree days](/topics/degree-days) are used to time their stages. UW–Madison uses a base of 52 °F and an upper limit of 86 °F, counting from the first egg mass: eggs take about 120 degree days to hatch, larvae reach the pupal stage at about 400 and new adults appear at about 675.[^uw-vegento-cpb][^a3422-2026]

## Damage

Young larvae feed on the new leaves at the top of the plant. The third and fourth instars do most of the damage, stripping whole leaves and then whole plants; the last instar alone eats up to three-quarters of the total.[^uw-vegento-cpb][^schuh-2026]

How much feeding potatoes can stand depends on their growth stage. Plants before flowering can lose 20 to 30 % of their leaf area without economic loss, flowering plants only 5 to 10 %, and plants setting and bulking tubers less than 10 %.[^a3422-2026] Eggplant tolerates much less feeding, and beetles can strip young transplants in days.[^uw-vegento-cpb]

## Monitoring

- Start in early May, once potatoes emerge or eggplant is transplanted, with the border rows next to last year's solanaceous crops and unmanaged ground.[^uw-vegento-cpb]
- Scan plants and the soil beneath them for adults, which drop when disturbed, and turn leaves for egg masses. Count both on a set number of plants to follow the trend.[^uw-vegento-cpb]
- Once adults or eggs are found, part the new leaves at the plant tops to look for small larvae. Scattered shot holes with no larvae nearby are usually flea beetle feeding.[^uw-vegento-cpb]
- UW–Madison's scouting calendar has the overwintered adults and their offspring from early May through June, both broods in July, and the first-generation adults and their offspring from August to mid-September. Since the generations overlap, keep looking for every stage all season.[^uw-vegento-cpb][^a3422-2026]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Rotation:** plant potatoes and other solanaceous crops away from last year's fields. The University of Minnesota suggests at least ¼ mile; the farther the better, since beetles that can't find a host in spring can't build up. Control solanaceous weeds too.[^uw-vegento-cpb][^schuh-2026]
- **Trap crops:** extra rows of potatoes planted next to overwintering sites, two weeks ahead of the main crop, draw in colonizing beetles, which can then be killed by flaming, vacuuming or flail chopping.[^uw-vegento-cpb]
- **Barriers:** plastic-lined trenches between fields and overwintering sites trap walking beetles; row covers, on from before emergence through peak colonization in mid-June, protect small plantings.[^uw-vegento-cpb]
- **Hand picking:** in gardens, crush egg masses and knock larvae and adults into soapy water every few days until colonization ends.[^uw-vegento-cpb][^schuh-2026]
- **Natural enemies:** predators, parasitic flies and wasps, and the fungus _Beauveria bassiana_ kill beetles but rarely keep numbers down in large fields.[^uw-vegento-cpb]

**Insecticides:** most commercial potato growers apply a systemic insecticide at planting, on the seed piece or in the furrow: a neonicotinoid (IRAC group 4A) or the diamide cyantraniliprole (group 28). The beetle has a long record of resistance: a high proportion of populations are resistant to the neonicotinoids, pyrethroids, carbamates and organophosphates, though Wisconsin beetles are still susceptible to many insecticides.[^uw-vegento-cpb-management][^a3422-2026] Foliar sprays against the first generation target small larvae and are timed to egg hatch: novaluron (group 15) and ledprona (group 35) from about 10 % hatch; spinosad and spinetoram (group 5), abamectin (group 6) and tolfenpyrad (group 21A) at about half hatch, often followed by two or three more applications 7 to 10 days apart.[^uw-vegento-cpb-management] Minnesota and Penn State time sprays at 15 to 30 % hatch.[^schuh-2026][^fronk-2024] Against the second generation, diamides (group 28) and isocycloseram (group 30) go on after the summer adults emerge, once defoliation reaches 5 to 10 %.[^uw-vegento-cpb-management]

To slow resistance, never use the same insecticide group on two generations in a row, including an at-plant diamide followed by a diamide spray in summer. Summer adults become next spring's colonizers, so count last year's applications too.[^uw-vegento-cpb] Avoid late-season sprays that select for resistance in beetles going into the winter.[^fronk-2024]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [management guide](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/resources/colorado-potato-beetle-management/) and [Penn State's page](https://extension.psu.edu/protect-potato-yields-by-managing-colorado-potato-beetles) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see the [University of Minnesota's page](https://extension.umn.edu/yard-and-garden-insects/colorado-potato-beetles).

## AgWeather's models

The [Colorado potato beetle model](/models/colorado-potato-beetle) shows a typical count of adults, egg masses and larvae through the season, from curves UW–Madison's Vegetable Entomology lab fitted to scouting counts in central Wisconsin potato fields, mostly in the Central Sands, 2014 to 2025. It counts degree days on 52 °F and 86 °F from January 1: overwintered adults colonize fields at about 205 degree days, the first egg masses appear at about 215, larvae show in sweep counts from about 400 and peak near 735; summer adults peak near 1,450. UW's stage table counts from the first egg mass instead: its 120 degree days to hatch put the first hatch near 335, a little before larvae show in sweeps.[^agweather-cpb][^uw-vegento-cpb] The same curves can be read by calendar date, as a typical central Wisconsin season. Tested on years left out of the fit, the curves timed the beetle's stages no better by degree days than by the calendar in these fields, where colonization and the peaks varied little from year to year; degree days should carry the timing better to places farther north or south, which these data can't test. The model's risk compares the count of adults and larvae with its season peak. A field's counts can run well above or below the typical ones, and outside central Wisconsin, and in tomato and eggplant, the model comes with a warning.[^agweather-cpb]
