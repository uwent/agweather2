The stalk borer (_Papaipema nebris_), also called the common stalk borer, is a moth whose caterpillars tunnel into the stems of young corn. They start life in grasses and weeds and move into crops when they outgrow them, so damage is usually heaviest in the rows next to fencerows, grassy terraces and waterways.[^a3646-2025] UW Vegetable Entomology lists it as a major pest of sweet corn.[^uw-vegento-crops] It also attacks soybeans, and occasionally tomatoes, peppers and potatoes.[^vt-sb]

## Identification

- **Young caterpillars** are cream to brownish with white stripes running along the body and a dark purple band around the middle, just behind the legs.[^purdue-sb][^iowa-sb][^vt-sb]
- **Full-grown caterpillars** lose the stripes and band, turn a plain gray and reach about 1¼ to 2 inches.[^purdue-sb][^iowa-sb]
- **Adults** are grayish brown moths, with or without small pale spots on the forewings, and a wingspan of 1 to 1½ inches.[^purdue-sb][^iowa-sb]

## Life cycle

There is one generation a year. Moths lay eggs from late summer until frost, mostly on grasses such as smooth brome, rye and wheat, and on giant ragweed, one of the few broadleaf weeds they favor.[^a3646-2025][^iowa-sb][^purdue-sb] The eggs spend the winter there and hatch in April and May. The young caterpillars bore into grass and weed stems; when they outgrow them, or when the weeds are killed, they move to larger plants such as corn.[^iowa-sb][^levine-1983] Caterpillars feed for two to four months, then pupate in the soil, and the moths emerge from August through October.[^iowa-sb][^vt-sb]

Development follows [degree days](/topics/degree-days) at a base of 41 °F counted from January 1.[^iowa-sb][^levine-1983] Egg hatch begins at about 575 and is done by about 750.[^iowa-sb] UW–Madison's field crops guide puts the first movement from grassy areas into corn at about 1,110 degree days and its peak at about 1,650.[^a3646-2025] Iowa State counts 10 % of caterpillars moving at 1,400 and half at about 1,700.[^iowa-sb] In AgWeather's long-term averages (1996–2025), 1,110 falls around June 12 at Arlington and 1,650 around July 1; at Rhinelander, about two weeks later.

## Damage

- **Leaf feeding:** the first sign is a row of small holes across the leaves of young corn, larger and more ragged than corn borer feeding.[^a3646-2025][^iowa-sb]
- **Stem boring:** caterpillars tunnel into the stem above ground, killing the central leaves ("dead heart"). Damaged plants may be stunted or twisted, or produce no ear. Once wilting is widespread, it is too late to spray.[^a3646-2025][^purdue-sb][^iowa-sb]
- **Where:** usually in the first four to eight rows beside the field margin. Patches farther into a field mark grassy weeds that escaped control the year before; caterpillars hatching there start feeding on corn earlier, since corn is all that is left.[^a3646-2025]
- **Crop stage:** small corn is most at risk; once plants reach about the seventh leaf they are unlikely to be killed.[^a3646-2025][^iowa-sb]

## Monitoring

- Scout border rows when about 1,300 to 1,400 degree days have accumulated, looking for caterpillars in the whorls and frass at entry holes.[^a3646-2025][^iowa-sb]
- Start with fields that had stalk borers before, especially edges next to grassy borders; dead grass heads nearby are another sign.[^iowa-sb] In tilled fields, check 20 plants at each of five places along fencerows, ditch banks, waterways and last year's giant ragweed patches.[^purdue-sb]
- Map damaged patches at harvest and check them the next year from about 900 to 1,000 degree days.[^a3646-2025]
- UW–Madison's guide gives action thresholds, adapted from Iowa State, by leaf stage, corn price and expected yield: the share of infested plants that justifies a spray is lowest for the youngest corn and rises steeply by the seventh leaf.[^a3646-2025][^iowa-sb]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Weed control** is the best long-term tactic: change herbicides, rotate crops or add cultivation to control the grassy weeds where eggs are laid.[^a3646-2025] Mow, burn or spray field margins, terraces and waterways before late summer, or in late winter.[^iowa-sb]
- **Early planting** helps corn outgrow damage.[^iowa-sb]
- **Natural enemies** such as ants, ground beetles and spiders eat caterpillars but rarely give much control.[^iowa-sb]
- **Home gardens:** mow or destroy large-stemmed weeds around the garden before and after planting. Split a damaged stem above the hole to remove the caterpillar, then tie the stem together. No insecticide controls stalk borers in home gardens.[^vt-sb]

**Bt corn:** some hybrids control or suppress stalk borers.[^iowa-sb]

**Insecticides** work only while caterpillars are moving and before they bore in; there is no rescue once they are inside the stem.[^iowa-sb][^purdue-sb] UW–Madison's guide times foliar sprays between 1,400 and 1,700 degree days, early in the move from grass into corn, and lists pyrethroids (IRAC group 3A, such as esfenvalerate and beta-cyfluthrin).[^a3646-2025] Where caterpillars come in from the edges, treating the border rows may be enough.[^iowa-sb][^purdue-sb] In reduced-till or weedy fields, a burndown herbicide pushes caterpillars from the weeds into the corn. Pair it with a contact insecticide, tank-mixed with a fast-acting herbicide or applied once the grass starts to die after a slow one.[^purdue-sb][^iowa-sb]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf) for field corn and [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/) for sweet corn, or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). Iowa State's [stalk borer page](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/encyclopedia/stalk-borer) and Purdue's [stalk borer page](https://ag.purdue.edu/department/entm/extension/field-crops-ipm/corn/stalk-borers.html) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see Virginia Cooperative Extension's [stalk borer page](https://pubs.ext.vt.edu/3104/3104-1567/3104-1567.html).

## AgWeather's models

The [stalk borer model](/models/stalk-borer) times caterpillars moving from grasses and weeds into corn, from degree days at a base of 41 °F with no upper limit counted from January 1.[^levine-1983][^agweather-sb] It follows the Iowa field study behind UW–Madison's and Iowa State's numbers: the first caterpillars move at about 1,100 degree days, 10 % by about 1,390 and half by about 1,650.[^rice-davis-2010][^iowa-sb][^a3646-2025] It marks a window from about 1,300 to 1,700 degree days to scout border rows and, if needed, spray. In AgWeather's long-term averages (1996–2025), the window opens around June 19 at Arlington, June 23 at Hancock and July 1 at Rhinelander. The model hasn't been compared with observations in Wisconsin.[^agweather-sb]
