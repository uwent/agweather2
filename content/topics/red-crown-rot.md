Red crown rot is a soybean disease caused by the soilborne fungus _Calonectria ilicicola_, which also attacks peanut and alfalfa. For decades it was known in the United States only in the Southeast. It was found in Illinois in 2018 and has since been confirmed in Kentucky, first in 2021, and in several other Midwestern states.[^mahecha-2025][^bradley-2023] In Wisconsin it was first confirmed in 2025, in one soybean field in Columbia County, where the affected area was about 10 feet across.[^smith-2026][^malvick-2025] The Crop Protection Network keeps a [map of confirmed counties](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/maps/red-crown-rot-map).[^cpn-rcr-map]

## Identification

Red crown rot is easy to mistake for other diseases, so look at the roots and lower stem, not just the leaves.

- **Leaves** show yellow flecks between the veins that grow into yellow and then brown, dead patches while the veins stay green. Leaf symptoms usually appear from early pod set (R3), sometimes as early as flowering (R1). They look much like sudden death syndrome, brown stem rot and southern stem canker.[^cpn-rcr][^bradley-2023]
- **The lower stem**, just above the soil line, turns reddish to reddish brown, and the roots rot. Plants may be stunted, and the roots and lower stem can show symptoms before the leaves do.[^cpn-rcr][^bradley-2023]
- **The telltale sign** comes late in the season: clusters of tiny, round, red to reddish-orange fruiting bodies (less than 1/16 inch across) on the lower stem and roots, often with white fungal growth.[^bradley-2023][^cpn-rcr] Other fungi can produce similar structures, and plants are often infected by more than one disease at a time, so confirm the cause with a laboratory test.[^mahecha-2025]

Affected plants usually occur in patches, often in low-lying, poorly drained or compacted parts of the field.[^cpn-rcr][^mahecha-2025]

## Life cycle

The fungus survives in soil and in old soybean roots as tiny resting structures called microsclerotia, which can last several years without a host. Moving soil and plant debris spread them, on equipment or in the wind.[^cpn-rcr][^smith-2026]

The fungus can infect roots within about two weeks of planting, but symptoms usually don't show until after flowering.[^ochi-2026] Root infection is greatest when the soil is between 77 and 86 °F,[^bradley-2023] and moist, warm soil favors the disease; warm soil early in the season lets infection start early.[^smith-2026] Leaf symptoms come from a toxin the fungus produces, which warm, wet weather after early pod set can bring on.[^cpn-rcr]

In a study of 242 fields in northern Japan, the disease was more common after a wet July, during vegetative growth (more than about 8 inches of rain), and after a hot August, during pod and seed development (average temperature above about 79 °F), with less than about 4 inches of rain. A field's history of red crown rot also counted.[^ochi-2026]

## Damage

Root and crown rot stunts and wilts plants, and badly affected plants die early while their neighbors stay green.[^mahecha-2025][^bradley-2023] All parts of yield suffer: the number of pods, the number of seeds and the seed weight.[^smith-2026] In patches measured in Kentucky fields, yields were about 70 % lower than in unaffected parts of the same fields.[^bradley-2023] The fungus lasts for years in the soil, so a field where it has been found stays at risk.[^cpn-rcr]

## Monitoring

- From late summer, look closely at patches of yellowing or dying plants. Don't assume they have sudden death syndrome: dig up a few plants and check the lower stem and roots for red discoloration and the small red fruiting bodies.[^smith-2026]
- Pay particular attention to low spots and heavier soils.[^smith-2026]
- Send whole plants, dug with their roots, to a diagnostic laboratory for confirmation. In Wisconsin, that's the UW–Madison [Plant Disease Diagnostics Clinic](https://pddc.wisc.edu/).[^pddc][^mahecha-2025]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Rotate** out of soybean, alfalfa and peanut for at least two years.[^cpn-rcr][^bradley-2023] Alfalfa is a host too, so take care with alfalfa in the rotation if red crown rot spreads in Wisconsin.[^smith-2026]
- **Clean equipment** before moving from an affected field to others, so soil and debris don't carry the fungus.[^mahecha-2025][^cpn-rcr]
- **Improve drainage** where water stands, avoid compaction and keep plants vigorous with good fertility and pest control.[^cpn-rcr][^mahecha-2025]
- **Plant into soil cooler than 77 °F** to reduce early infection, and manage soybean cyst nematode, which does more harm together with red crown rot than either does alone.[^bradley-2023]
- **Varieties** differ in susceptibility, but none is marketed as resistant.[^cpn-rcr][^bradley-2023]

**Fungicides:** Seed treatments with the active ingredients fluopyram or pydiflumetofen (FRAC group 7)[^a3646-2025] have supplemental labels for suppression of red crown rot.[^mahecha-2025] A seed treatment labeled for red crown rot can help protect against early infection, though results vary.[^bradley-2023][^cpn-rcr] Foliar fungicides are not labeled for red crown rot and don't control it.[^bradley-2023]

For products and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf), which lists these seed treatments for sudden death syndrome and soybean cyst nematode, and check each label for red crown rot. The [Crop Protection Network's red crown rot page](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/encyclopedia/red-crown-rot-of-soybean) and the University of Kentucky's [fact sheet](https://plantpathology.ca.uky.edu/files/ppfs-ag-s-25.pdf) explain the choices in more detail, and UW–Madison's [field crop disease update](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/articles/2026-mid-season-wisconsin-field-crop-disease-update/) describes the disease in Wisconsin.

## AgWeather's models

The [red crown rot model](/models/red-crown-rot) estimates the share of plants in a field that will show symptoms from the weather around flowering: the average temperature in the month after R1, and the rain in the month before and the month after. It starts at R1 from the [soybean stages model](/models/soybean-stages), or at your own date at a location.[^agweather-rcr] It gives a value once the month after R1 is complete. It comes from the Japanese field study above, in fields nearly all infested and in warmer summers than Wisconsin's, and hasn't been tested in the United States, so it shows the predicted incidence without a risk level: the Crop Protection Network's algorithm sets no risk levels yet. It doesn't know a field's history, which the study found the strongest factor, and it applies only where the pathogen is in the field. Since nothing can be done in season, read it for awareness and planning (rotation, variety, seed treatment), not as a spray decision.[^agweather-rcr]
