The cabbage looper (_Trichoplusia ni_) is a moth whose caterpillars feed on cabbage, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cauliflower and the other cole crops, and also on beets, celery, lettuce, peas, potatoes, spinach and tomatoes.[^uw-hort] Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin lists loopers among the insects to control in celery, hops, leafy greens, melon, mint, pea, potato and tomato as well as cole crops.[^a3422-2026] With the [imported cabbageworm](/topics/imported-cabbageworm) and the diamondback moth, it is one of the three main caterpillar pests of Wisconsin cole crops, which growers scout for together.[^uw-vegento][^a3422-2026] It can't survive winters in the upper Midwest; each year's loopers arrive as moths migrating from the south.[^umn-caterpillars][^uw-hort]

## Identification

- **Adults** are night-flying moths, grayish brown and about 1½ inches across the wings. Near the center of each mottled forewing is a small silvery-white mark that looks like a figure 8 or a Y.[^uw-hort][^umn-caterpillars]
- **Eggs** are round, creamy white and about the size of a pinhead, laid singly on the undersides of lower leaves.[^uw-vegento][^umn-caterpillars]
- **Larvae** are pale green caterpillars with a thin white line along each side, growing to 1 to 1½ inches. They have no legs in the middle of the body, so they arch into a loop as they crawl.[^uw-vegento][^umn-caterpillars]

When disturbed, a looper inches away or whips its head around, while an imported cabbageworm usually stays still and a diamondback moth larva wriggles.[^uw-vegento]

## Life cycle

Moths migrate into Wisconsin from mid-July through September, according to UW–Madison; the University of Minnesota gives early July to late August for Minnesota.[^uw-hort][^umn-caterpillars] Of the three caterpillar pests, loopers tend to show up last, after the diamondback moth and the imported cabbageworm.[^mwvg-2026]

A female lays 200 to 300 eggs, usually over 10 to 12 days, and they hatch in three to six days. Caterpillars feed for up to five weeks before pupating in a loose silk cocoon, and moths emerge 10 to 14 days later.[^uw-vegento] Their offspring, the second generation in Wisconsin, do the most damage to cole crops.[^uw-hort] Depending on when the moths arrive and how warm late summer is, there can be one to three generations a season.[^umn-caterpillars]

Like other insects, loopers develop faster in warm weather. In laboratory tests, loopers didn't develop at all at 45 °F (7.2 °C) and none reached adulthood after being held at 50 °F (10 °C); a few did after being held at 55 °F (12.7 °C), but most of those were deformed.[^toba-1973]

## Damage

Caterpillars feed between the veins, often on the undersides of lower leaves, and large ones chew big, ragged holes.[^uw-vegento][^umn-caterpillars] Heavy feeding stunts cabbage and cauliflower heads, and damage to the bud of a young cabbage can keep a head from forming. Loopers also bore into the heads of early cabbage, through three to six layers of wrapper leaves, making them unmarketable.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento] Their greenish-brown droppings (frass) contaminate heads and leaves.[^uw-hort]

## Monitoring

- Scout weekly through the season. Walk a W-shaped path and examine 25 to 50 plants, checking lower leaves and leaf undersides for eggs and caterpillars. Count a plant as infested if it has eggs or caterpillars of any of the three species.[^a3422-2026][^uw-hort]
- Treat when the share of infested plants passes the threshold for the crop's stage. For cabbage: 10 % in the seedbed, 30 % from transplanting to cupping, 20 % from cupping to early head and 10 % at mature head. For broccoli and cauliflower: 10 % in the seedbed, 50 % from transplanting to the first flower bud or curd, and 10 % after.[^a3422-2026]
- Pheromone traps show when moths arrive. Because arrival varies from year to year, degree-day models for loopers developed elsewhere start counting from the first moth caught in a trap.[^agweather-cabbage-looper]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Clean transplants:** start with transplants free of caterpillars and eggs.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento]
- **Natural enemies** often keep loopers in check. Predators such as paper wasps and lady beetles, and parasitic wasps and flies, attack the caterpillars.[^uw-hort][^umn-caterpillars]
- **Sanitation:** turn under crop residue after harvest, rotate cole crops between fields and remove mustard-family weeds.[^uw-vegento][^umn-caterpillars]
- **Row covers or netting** keep moths from laying eggs in small plantings.[^uw-hort][^mwvg-2026]

**Insecticides** work best on small caterpillars, with good coverage of the leaves.[^uw-hort][^umn-caterpillars] Selective products spare natural enemies: _Bacillus thuringiensis_ (Bt; IRAC group 11A) subspecies _kurstaki_ or _aizawai_, spinosyns (group 5; spinosad, which is listed for organic production, and spinetoram) and insect growth regulators (group 18; methoxyfenozide, tebufenozide).[^uw-hort][^a3422-2026][^mwvg-2026] Other classes listed for the caterpillar complex include diamides (group 28; chlorantraniliprole, cyantraniliprole), avermectins (group 6; emamectin benzoate), indoxacarb (group 22A) and pyrethroids (group 3A), which also kill natural enemies.[^a3422-2026][^umn-caterpillars][^irac-moa] Resistance is a concern with all caterpillars on cole crops, so rotate classes.[^uw-hort]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [cabbage looper page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/cabbage-looper/) and the University of Minnesota's [caterpillars on cole crops page](https://extension.umn.edu/yard-and-garden-insects/caterpillars-cole-crops) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see UW–Madison's [cabbage looper fact sheet](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/cabbage-looper/).

## AgWeather's models

The [cabbage looper model](/models/cabbage-looper) is USPest.org's cabbage looper model, built on laboratory development times.[^uspest-cbl][^toba-1973] It counts degree days at a base of 50 °F with an upper limit of 90 °F from the first moth caught in a pheromone trap. Without a trap date it starts on July 15, when UW–Madison says the moths begin arriving; that's a typical arrival, not a forecast, and in Minnesota moths can come in early July.[^uw-hort][^umn-caterpillars] Egg laying begins about 120 degree days after the first catch, caterpillars hatch about 100 degree days after an egg is laid and feed for about 420, and a new generation starts every 912 degree days until fall freezes end the season. The risk is the caterpillars feeding, with the second generation's counted most since UW–Madison says it does the most damage, and a window marks when small caterpillars are hatching.[^agweather-cabbage-looper]

On AgWeather's long-term averages (degree days for 1996 to 2025) and the July 15 start, the first generation's caterpillars peak in early to mid-August, and the second generation, which UW–Madison says does the most damage, lays its eggs in early to mid-September in southern and central Wisconsin.[^agweather-cabbage-looper][^uw-hort] We found no Wisconsin trap or scouting records to check the timing against, and moths that arrive later in the summer can lay eggs outside the model's windows. If you trap, enter your first catch; either way, base sprays on your own scouting.
