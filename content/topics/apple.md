The apple (_Malus domestica_) is the most common tree fruit grown in Wisconsin, in commercial orchards and home plantings. An orchard is a long-term investment: a planting should last 20 to 25 years. In spring, much of an apple grower's work is timed by the trees' growth stage, because the main diseases and insects strike at particular stages. This page describes those stages and the weather risks of Wisconsin's season, and links to the pests and diseases AgWeather models.[^wisconsin-apples][^roper-2004]

## Apples in Wisconsin

Winter cold limits where apples do well in Wisconsin. Where winter lows routinely drop below −35 °F, the cold can kill flower buds and even whole trees. Most of the state is in USDA hardiness zones 4 and 5, where apples grow well on a good site. Where the state reaches into zone 3, growing apples is marginal, and choosing the site matters even more.[^roper-2004]

Spring frost is the other limit: below 28 °F, flower buds, open flowers and young fruit can be killed. Cold air is heavy and pools in low ground, so on a calm, frosty night a valley or coulee can be several degrees colder than the hillside above it. A site raised above the land around it, on a slope, lets cold air drain away from the trees.[^roper-2004]

Spring warmth builds at different rates across the state. Southern Wisconsin usually warms well ahead of Door County and the north, so each stage comes later there.[^dillon-2026][^agweather-codling-moth] Within an orchard, early cultivars such as Zestar reach bloom a few days ahead of later ones such as Fuji, Honeycrisp and McIntosh.[^dillon-2026][^agweather-apple-stages]

## Growth stages

- **Silver tip:** buds swell, and silvery, fuzzy leaf tissue shows at the tip.
- **Green tip:** green leaf tissue shows at the bud tips.
- **Half-inch green:** leaves stand out about half an inch from the bud.
- **Tight cluster:** the flower buds are visible, still tightly grouped among the young leaves.
- **Pink:** the flower buds have separated and show pink, but haven't opened.
- **Bloom:** the center (king) blossom of each cluster opens first; at full bloom, at least 80 % of flowers are open.
- **Petal fall:** most petals have dropped.
- **Fruit set:** the young fruit is set and growing.[^steil-2024]

Stages follow warmth, so [degree days](/topics/degree-days) predict them better than calendar dates. Wisconsin's fruit scouting reports give apple degree days above 43 °F from January 1 alongside the stages they observe. Degree days don't reliably predict bud break, though, which matters most after a warm winter, so check your own buds.[^dillon-2026][^atucha-2024]

## What the stages mean for pests and diseases

- **Green tip to a few weeks after petal fall:** rain releases the spring spores of [apple scab](/topics/apple-scab), and young leaves and fruit are most susceptible.[^peter-2026]
- **Bloom:** open blossoms can be infected by [fire blight](/topics/fire-blight), most readily in warm weather with rain or heavy dew. Early cultivars bloom first and are at risk first.[^holland-2026][^dillon-2026]
- **Bloom, for pollinators:** bees pollinate the crop, so don't spray insecticides during bloom or while bees are foraging.[^dillon-2026]
- **Full bloom on:** the first [codling moths](/topics/codling-moth) fly from around full bloom, and their eggs begin hatching about 250 degree days (base 50 °F) after the biofix, the date set from the first moths caught in traps.[^van-zoeren-2018]

Other pests to scout for in spring include mites, aphids, San Jose scale and tarnished plant bug. Wisconsin's orchard trap network follows apple maggot, dogwood borer, lesser peachtree borer and two leafrollers along with codling moth.[^dillon-2026][^datcp-traps]

## Monitoring

- Record the dates your own trees reach green tip, first bloom and petal fall. Disease and insect models count from these dates.[^dillon-2026]
- Read the UW–Madison [Wisconsin Fruit News](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/wisconsin-fruit-news/) in season for scouting reports, degree days and disease updates.
- Follow the weekly [trap counts](https://datcp.wi.gov/Pages/Programs_Services/AppleOrchardPests.aspx) from Wisconsin's Department of Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection (DATCP).

## Management

Many problems are settled at planting: an orchard can't be moved off a frost-prone site, and resistant cultivars and rootstocks reduce the damage from scab and fire blight.[^ayer-2020] Pruning for an open canopy helps leaves dry and sprays reach them.[^ayer-2020] For spray programs, see the [Midwest Fruit Pest Management Guide](https://edustore.purdue.edu/id-465-w.html); for site selection, cultivars, rootstocks and pruning, see UW–Madison's [apple resources](https://fruit.wisc.edu/tree-fruit/apples/apple-resources/) and [Planning and Establishing Commercial Apple Orchards in Wisconsin](http://fruit.webhosting.cals.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/36/2011/06/Planning-and-Establishing-Commercial-Apple-Orchards-in-Wisconsin-2.pdf).

## AgWeather's models

- The [apple stages model](/models/apple-stages) estimates each day's stage, from silver tip to fruit set, from degree days. It follows McIntosh; early cultivars such as Zestar run a few days ahead.
- The [apple scab model](/models/apple-scab) marks infection periods from green tip through the spring spore season.
- The [fire blight model](/models/fire-blight) gives the risk of blossom infection during bloom.
- The [codling moth model](/models/codling-moth) times moth flight and egg hatch from your first trap catch, or from one estimated for your latitude.

At a location, you can enter your own green tip and first bloom dates, and they replace the estimates from degree days.
