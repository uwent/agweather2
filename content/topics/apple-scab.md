Apple scab is a disease caused by the fungus _Venturia inaequalis_. It infects apple, crabapple, hawthorn and mountain-ash; pear gets a similar disease from a related fungus. Scab is most severe in cool, wet springs, and in such years it can be widespread across Wisconsin.[^ayer-2020][^hudelson-2026][^spangenberg-2025]

## Identification

- **On leaves**, where scab is often noticed first, spots are most common on the upper surface. They are roughly circular with feathery edges, from pinhead size to about ½ inch across. Young spots are light olive green and look velvety as the fungus produces spores; they darken to brown or black. Badly spotted leaves turn yellow and drop, often from July on.[^hudelson-2026][^ayer-2020][^spangenberg-2025]
- **On fruit**, the spots look much the same. As the fruit grows they can crack and misshape it, and scabbed fruit is hard to sell and stores poorly.[^ayer-2020]

## Life cycle

The fungus spends the winter in infected leaves on the orchard floor. Small fruiting bodies form in these leaves, and spring spores called ascospores mature in them over 4 to 6 weeks. From green tip (when green leaf tissue first shows at the bud tips) until a few weeks after petal fall, rain releases the mature ascospores and wind carries them to young leaves and fruit. These spring infections are called primary infections.[^peter-2026][^ayer-2020]

An ascospore infects only if the leaf or fruit stays wet long enough. Cooler weather needs longer: about 28 hours of wetness at 39 °F, but only 6 hours at 61 to 75 °F. Spots appear 9 to 17 days later, sooner in warm weather. Each spot then produces a second kind of spore, called conidia, which rain and wind spread to new leaves and fruit. These secondary infections can repeat all summer.[^peter-2026]

The weather while the leaves unfold does much to decide how bad a year's scab will be: cold, wet weather then favors it, and dry, warm weather doesn't.[^spangenberg-2025] Wet, mild weather at bloom can raise the risk of scab and fire blight at the same time.[^holland-2026]

## Damage

Leaf loss weakens trees, reduces yield and can reduce the next year's bloom.[^ayer-2020] Spots on the fruit lower its grade or make it unmarketable. Young leaves and young fruit are the most susceptible, so the primary season matters most. Once scab has a start in the orchard, it is much harder to bring under control, and in a wet spring one unprotected infection period can be enough.[^holland-2026]

## Monitoring

- Note the date of green tip in your own orchard, and the date of petal fall. Weather models count from these dates.[^dillon-2026]
- Follow infection periods for your area. Ascospore maturity and infection forecasts help you time sprays.[^ayer-2020]
- Scout leaves and fruit for spots, starting with susceptible cultivars. McIntosh, Cortland, Jersey Mac and Jonagold are highly susceptible; Honeycrisp is fairly resistant.[^ayer-2020]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls** reduce the spores that start each season:

- Shred fallen leaves with a flail mower, or apply urea to them, to speed their breakdown. In home plantings, rake up and remove fallen leaves in autumn.[^ayer-2020][^hudelson-2026]
- Prune for an open canopy, so leaves dry faster and sprays reach them.[^ayer-2020]
- Plant scab-resistant cultivars. Strains of the fungus that overcome the main resistance gene have appeared, so resistance isn't a guarantee.[^ayer-2020]

**Fungicides** protect new growth but don't cure leaves already infected. On susceptible cultivars, sprays may be needed from green tip until the primary season ends.[^spangenberg-2025][^ayer-2020]

- **Protectants** such as captan (FRAC group M04) and mancozeb (group M03) are the base of a program. Apply them before rain and keep new growth covered through the primary season.[^holland-2026][^frac-2026]
- **Single-site fungicides** are best tank-mixed with a protectant: demethylation inhibitors (DMIs, FRAC group 3; fenbuconazole, myclobutanil, mefentrifluconazole), succinate dehydrogenase inhibitors (SDHIs, group 7; penthiopyrad, pydiflumetofen) and anilinopyrimidines (group 9; cyprodinil, pyrimethanil). Quinone outside inhibitors (QoIs, group 11) are also among the most effective. Rotate groups, and pair them with a protectant, to slow the spread of resistant strains. DMIs and anilinopyrimidines give only limited control after an infection; if you find spots, lean on protectants.[^holland-2026][^ayer-2020]
- **Organic programs** rely on sulfur and lime sulfur (group M02), with potassium bicarbonate, which FRAC doesn't assign to a group.[^frac-2026] They wash off easily and act little after an infection, so spray before rain at short intervals. Lime sulfur can injure leaves in hot weather.[^holland-2026]

For products, timing and current registrations, see the [Midwest Fruit Pest Management Guide](https://edustore.purdue.edu/id-465-w.html). UW–Madison's [Wisconsin apple disease update](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/wisconsin-apple-disease-update-high-risk-conditions-in-wisconsin/) and Cornell's [apple scab fact sheet](https://cals.cornell.edu/integrated-pest-management/outreach-education/fact-sheets/apple-scab-fruit-fact-sheet) explain the choices in more detail; for home trees, see UW–Madison's [apple scab fact sheet](https://pddc.wisc.edu/2018/11/19/apple-scab/).

## AgWeather's models

The [apple scab model](/models/apple-scab) marks infection periods from green tip until the season's ascospores are spent, and estimates how many ascospores are mature and released. It starts at the green tip date from the [apple stages model](/models/apple-stages), or at your own date at a location. We estimate leaf wetness from the weather rather than measuring it, so the model tends to show more infection periods than a sensor in your orchard would. It covers only primary infections, not the secondary infections of summer.
