The squash vine borer (_Melittia cucurbitae_) is a moth whose larvae feed inside the stems of squash and pumpkins. Its attacks are spotty from year to year, but in a bad year it can cause serious losses.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento] Winter squash, zucchini and pumpkins, including giant pumpkins, are among the most often attacked; Hubbard and acorn squash suffer more than butternut.[^uw-hort][^umn] Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin also lists it among the insects of cucumber and melon, though the University of Minnesota counts these among the vine crops it seldom attacks.[^a3422-2026][^umn]

## Identification

- **Adults** are clearwing moths that fly by day and are easily mistaken for wasps. The forewings are a metallic greenish brown and the hind wings clear. The body is orange with black dots or bands, and the hind legs carry tufts of orange and black hairs. The wings span 1 to 1½ inches.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento][^umn]
- **Eggs** are small, flat and brown, laid one at a time at the base of the plant.[^umn]
- **Larvae** are cream-colored caterpillars with brown heads, growing to about an inch long.[^uw-hort][^umn]

## Life cycle

The squash vine borer has one generation a year in Wisconsin.[^uw-vegento] It spends the winter as a pupa in a cocoon in the soil, and the moths come out in late June and July. UW–Madison's horticulture fact sheet times their emergence to about 900 [degree days](/topics/degree-days) above a base of 50 °F, counted from January 1, which comes about when chicory, the common blue-flowered roadside weed, is in full bloom.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento-models] UW Vegetable Entomology's page puts emergence a little later, after about 1,000 degree days.[^uw-vegento] Females lay their eggs at the base of host plants over about three weeks, centered on about 1,000 degree days; each can lay 150 to 200.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento] The larvae hatch after one to two weeks, bore straight into the stems and feed there for two to six weeks; UW–Madison's sources differ on how long. Grown larvae leave the stems and burrow into the soil to pupate.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento][^umn]

## Damage

The larvae tunnel through the center of the stem, cutting off water to the rest of the plant. The first sign is wilting in the heat of the day; plants wilted in the morning deserve a close look. Damage is easy to miss until plants collapse and die in late July and August.[^uw-hort][^umn] Larvae in the main stem can kill the whole plant, while one in a runner kills only that runner, which costs little on a large plant.[^uw-hort][^a3422-2026]

Wilt from bacterial wilt or Fusarium wilt can look the same. Look for holes near the base of wilting vines with sawdust-like droppings (frass) around them, and split the stem lengthwise to find the larvae.[^uw-hort] Fields damaged in past years are likely to be damaged again.[^uw-hort]

## Monitoring

- Start watching for moths from about 900 degree days, or the last week of June. Their colors, daytime flight and loud buzz make them easy to spot.[^uw-hort][^umn][^uw-vegento]
- Yellow pans or bowls filled with water and a drop of dish soap catch the moths, which are drawn to yellow. Set them out by late June and check them daily.[^uw-vegento][^umn]
- Pheromone traps catch male moths and show when the moths are flying, but they also catch related clearwing moths, so check identifications. Neither kind of trap has a treatment threshold.[^umn][^uw-vegento]
- Check the bases of stems for entry holes and frass, which show where larvae have bored in before plants wilt.[^uw-vegento]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Early planting:** plantings started early in the season tend to have fewer problems, and a second planting of summer squash in early July matures after the moths have finished laying eggs.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento][^umn]
- **Crop choice and trap crops:** less susceptible types such as butternut squash suffer less damage. An early, attractive trap crop such as Hubbard or summer squash draws egg laying away from the main crop; destroy it while the larvae are still inside the stems.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento][^umn]
- **Row covers** keep moths from laying eggs. Put them on when vines start to run or when moths are first seen, anchor the edges, and take them off when the crop flowers so bees can pollinate. Don't cover ground where cucurbits grew the year before, or moths emerging from the soil will be trapped underneath.[^umn][^uw-hort]
- **Rotation and sanitation:** move cucurbits away from last year's fields and destroy infested vines and crop residue before the larvae leave them to pupate.[^umn][^uw-vegento]
- **In small plantings**, larvae can be cut out of a slit stem; cover the wound with moist soil so the vine roots again.[^uw-hort][^umn]

**Insecticides** work only against newly hatched larvae before they enter the stem, so residue must be on the stems through the egg-laying period. UW–Madison recommends starting when moths are seen, at about 900 degree days, and repeating at 5- to 7-day intervals through the three weeks of egg laying, especially on plants with runners shorter than 2 feet. Once larvae are inside the vine, sprays do little.[^a3422-2026][^uw-hort] Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin lists pyrethroids (IRAC group 3A), neonicotinoids (group 4A; acetamiprid, and thiamethoxam in a premix with a pyrethroid) and diamides (group 28, only in premixes) for squash vine borer.[^a3422-2026][^irac-moa] _Bacillus thuringiensis_ (Bt, group 11A) doesn't work, because the larvae must eat it and they feed protected inside the stem.[^umn] Broad-spectrum insecticides can kill bees, so avoid them while the crop is flowering; if a spray can't wait, apply it in the evening, when bees are less active.[^umn]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [squash vine borer page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/squash-vine-borer/) and the University of Minnesota's [squash vine borer page](https://extension.umn.edu/yard-and-garden-insects/squash-vine-borers) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see UW–Madison's [squash vine borer fact sheet](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/squash-vine-borer/).

## AgWeather's models

The [squash vine borer model](/models/squash-vine-borer) counts degree days from January 1 at a base of 50 °F with no upper limit, and rates the risk of moth activity from 0 to 4 in one window from 750 to 1,000 degree days, peaking at 900. It comes from VDIFN, the forecasting tool AgWeather replaces, and gives the same ratings as VDIFN for the same degree days. No source is known for its numbers, and it hasn't been compared with trap catches in Wisconsin.[^agweather-squash-vine-borer]

The model's window is shorter and earlier than UW–Madison's advice. Its peak matches the 900 degree days at which UW–Madison's fact sheet expects the moths, but it ends at 1,000, about when the fact sheet puts the middle of three weeks of egg laying, and when UW Vegetable Entomology's page says the moths begin to emerge.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento][^agweather-squash-vine-borer] We worked out what this means on the calendar from AgWeather's long-term averages (degree days for 1996 to 2025): in an average year at Arlington the window runs from about June 21 to July 3, and at Rhinelander from about July 3 to July 18, while egg laying, by UW–Madison's timing, goes on for a week or more after each window closes. Michigan State's Enviroweather model, also without a cited source, has the flight lasting to 2,000 degree days.[^agweather-squash-vine-borer] AgWeather's model is the likelier to change. Until it does, use it to know when to start watching for moths, and keep stems protected for the three weeks of egg laying, whatever the model shows after its window ends.
