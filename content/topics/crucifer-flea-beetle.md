The crucifer flea beetle (_Phyllotreta cruciferae_) is a small leaf beetle that feeds on cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, radish, turnip, mustards, horseradish and the other crucifers.[^uw-vegento][^umass] It is one of several flea beetles common in Wisconsin, most of which feed on a narrow group of crops: the potato flea beetle on potato, tomato, eggplant and pepper, the eggplant flea beetle on eggplant, and the spinach flea beetle on spinach and beets.[^uw-vegento] The crucifer and striped flea beetles are the main species on cole crops in the Midwest.[^mwvg-2026]

## Identification

- **Adults** are shiny black, sometimes with a greenish or bluish sheen, and 1/16 to ⅛ inch long. Like all flea beetles, they have enlarged hind legs and jump when disturbed.[^uw-vegento][^umass][^umn]
- **The striped flea beetle** (_Phyllotreta striolata_), also found on cabbage, is black with two wavy yellow stripes down its back.[^uw-vegento][^umass]
- **Larvae** are slender and white with a brown head, and live in the soil, feeding on roots.[^uw-vegento]

## Life cycle

Adults spend the winter in leaf litter, crop debris and soil, in hedgerows, windbreaks and woods near fields.[^umn][^umass][^uw-vegento] They become active in early spring, once temperatures reach about 50 °F, and feed on weeds and early-planted crops; in Massachusetts they move into fields in early May.[^uw-vegento][^umass] They are most active on warm, calm, sunny days.[^uw-vegento]

Females lay eggs in the soil at the base of host plants in May. The larvae feed on root hairs for about a month, then pupate in the soil.[^uw-vegento][^umass] In Massachusetts, the new generation of adults appears in late June and a second in late July, overlapping so that crops are at risk whenever they are planted; beetles leave fields to overwinter in mid-September.[^umass] For flea beetles in general, the University of Minnesota gives one to two generations a year and UW Vegetable Entomology one to three, depending on the species.[^umn][^uw-vegento]

## Damage

Adults chew small round pits and holes, usually less than ⅛ inch across, from the underside of the leaf, giving it a shot-hole look. The thin upper surface left over a pit dries and falls out, and the holes grow as the leaf grows.[^uw-vegento][^umn][^umass] Seedlings can be killed outright, and heavy feeding on young plants slows them and lowers yield.[^umass][^a3422-2026] Plants grown from seed suffer more than transplants.[^umn] Larvae on the roots rarely cause economic damage.[^uw-vegento]

Flea beetles do the most harm in spring. By summer most plants have four or five leaves and can withstand feeding, and beetle numbers drop.[^umn] Leafy crops are the exception: in kale, bok choy, mustard and other greens, holes in the leaves can make the crop unmarketable.[^uw-vegento] Crops with glossy leaves and a sharp flavor, such as bok choy, Chinese cabbage and mustard, are especially attractive; waxy cabbage, broccoli and kale are fed on mostly along the leaf edges as they mature.[^umass]

## Monitoring

- Check new plantings for feeding damage every one to two days, since flea beetles can quickly destroy young plants. Damage is easier to spot than the beetles, which jump away.[^uw-vegento][^umn]
- Yellow sticky cards catch adults; the number caught reflects how much feeding is taking place nearby.[^uw-vegento]
- No threshold fits every cole crop. The Midwest guide suggests treating at about one beetle per plant on small plants and two to five on larger ones, using the lower numbers where beetles feed on the part you sell; the University of Minnesota suggests treating when 10 to 20 % of a stand shows feeding damage.[^mwvg-2026][^umn]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Row covers or netting,** laid over the crop at seeding or transplanting and sealed at the edges, keep beetles off young plants. Pair them with rotation, since beetles can spend the winter in the soil where they fed the year before.[^umass][^mwvg-2026][^uw-vegento]
- **Planting date and location:** plant spring crops away from last year's brassica fields and separate early and late plantings. Planting later lets plants grow quickly past the stage when they are most easily hurt.[^umass][^umn]
- **Trap crops:** a highly attractive crop, such as mustard or bok choy, planted 7 to 14 days before the main crop on about 10 % of the area, draws beetles where they can be treated.[^umn]
- **Sanitation:** control weeds and destroy crop residue promptly after harvest to remove food and overwintering cover.[^umn][^umass]
- **Natural enemies,** including a native parasitic wasp, lacewing larvae and other predators, and beneficial nematodes that attack larvae in the soil, give some control, though the parasitic wasp is uncommon.[^umass][^umn][^uw-vegento]

**Insecticides** may be needed as rescue treatments on seedlings and transplants.[^a3422-2026] UW Vegetable Entomology notes that soil-applied insecticides can protect plants through the season, while foliar sprays act quickly but may harm natural enemies of other cole crop pests.[^uw-vegento] Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin lists neonicotinoids (IRAC group 4A; clothianidin, dinotefuran, imidacloprid, thiamethoxam), applied to the soil or the leaves, a diamide (group 28; cyantraniliprole), pyrethroids (group 3A; bifenthrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, zeta-cypermethrin and others), a carbamate (group 1A; carbaryl) and tolfenpyrad (group 21A).[^a3422-2026] For organic production, spinosad (group 5) works best and pyrethrins (group 3A) and azadirachtin (group UN, mode of action uncertain) give some contact control.[^umass][^irac-moa] Use a spreader-sticker on waxy crops, and expect to reapply.[^umass] Kaolin clay gives mixed results.[^umn]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [flea beetles page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/flea-beetles/) and UMass's [flea beetle biology page](https://www.umass.edu/agriculture-food-environment/vegetable/fact-sheets/flea-beetle-biology) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see the University of Minnesota's [flea beetles page](https://extension.umn.edu/yard-and-garden-insects/flea-beetles).

## AgWeather's models

The [crucifer flea beetle model](/models/crucifer-flea-beetle) counts degree days from January 1 by the modified average method, base 50 °F with a cutoff at 86 °F, and follows the adults through the season. Its first stage is the overwintered beetles feeding on seedlings and transplants: UW–Madison's degree-day table puts flea beetles at 150 to 200 degree days, and in the model a tenth of the spring beetles are active by about 145 and activity peaks at 200.[^uw-degree-days] The beetles then lay eggs, and the new summer adults follow about 1,350 degree days after the spring peak, from the development times measured in southwestern Ontario.[^kinoshita-1979] Both kinds of adults count in the risk, and a window marks when to scout new plantings every one to two days.[^agweather-crucifer-flea-beetle][^uw-vegento]

On AgWeather's long-term averages (degree days for 1996 to 2025), the spring beetles are active from late April in southern Wisconsin and early May in the north, peaking in early to mid-May. The summer adults peak in late July in the south and early to mid-August in the north, later than the late June that UMass reports for Massachusetts.[^agweather-crucifer-flea-beetle][^umass] Their timing rests on estimates of when the spring beetles lay eggs, and the model hasn't been compared with counts in Wisconsin. Scout every spring planting from emergence whatever the model shows.
