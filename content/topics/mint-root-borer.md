The mint root borer (_Fumibotys fumalis_, also listed as _Pyrausta fumalis_) is a small moth whose caterpillars feed inside the rhizomes and on the roots of peppermint and spearmint. Where it builds up, it can severely thin mint stands.[^pnw] It is a recognized pest in most mint-growing areas, and UW–Madison's Vegetable Entomology lab lists a degree-day model for it among Wisconsin's vegetable insects.[^pnw][^uw-vegento-models] In Wisconsin, where mint is grown on muck and other high-organic soils or on fine-textured soils, the pest is little studied: Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin doesn't list it among the insects of mint, and most guidance comes from the Pacific Northwest.[^uw-vegento-crops][^a3422-2026][^pnw]

## Identification

- **Adults** are small moths, active in the field from late spring to midsummer. They can be caught with sweep nets and with sticky traps baited with the female's sex pheromone.[^pnw]
- **Young larvae** are light green to yellow, with dark stripes down the back.[^pnw]
- **Older larvae** are yellow to tan with a brown head, and grow to about ¾ inch long. They are found inside rhizomes and in the soil among the roots.[^pnw]

## Life cycle

The mint root borer has one generation a year. It spends the winter as a full-grown larva (a prepupa) in a cocoon in the soil around the mint roots, pupates in spring, and flies as a moth from May through July.[^pnw] In Oregon State's degree-day model, counted from April 1 at a base of 50 °F, the first moths are caught at about 430 [degree days](/topics/degree-days), moth catches peak at 910, egg laying at 1,130 and egg hatch at 1,280. From about 1,850 degree days the grown larvae begin to make the chambers (hibernacula) in which they spend the winter, and half have done so by 2,150.[^uspest-mrb] The larvae feed inside rhizomes and on roots from late July through September, and into early October in some years.[^pnw]

## Damage

The larvae tunnel in the rhizomes and feed on the roots, which can severely thin the stand.[^pnw]

## Monitoring

- **Larvae:** sample after harvest, from late August through mid-September, when most larvae are big enough to find but haven't yet done much damage. Take square-foot samples of soil to the depth of the rhizomes, sift them, and count the larvae.[^pnw]
- **Threshold:** the Pacific Northwest handbook suggests treating at two to four larvae per square foot, depending on the age and vigor of the field, other pests and stresses, and the price of oil.[^pnw]
- **Moths:** sweep nets and pheromone-baited sticky traps show when moths are flying. There is no threshold for moths, but catches, together with a field's history, help plan control of eggs and young larvae before damage is done.[^pnw]

## Management

**Field practices:** Wisconsin growers usually plow mint shallowly once the first killing frost has come, partly to control pests that would otherwise survive the winter on the stubble, and mint is grown in short rotations of about three years in mint and three in another crop.[^uw-vegento-crops] We found no measure of how much they reduce mint root borer.

**Insecticides:** the Pacific Northwest handbook lists a diamide (IRAC group 28; chlorantraniliprole, which may be applied through irrigation water), a diamide and neonicotinoid premix (groups 28 and 4A), azadirachtin (group UN, mode of action uncertain), with some formulations allowed in organic production, and an organophosphate soil treatment (group 1B; ethoprop) applied once, before planting or after the last harvest, and worked into the soil.[^pnw][^irac-moa] Check that any product is registered for mint in Wisconsin before you use it.

For products, rates and current registrations, see the Pacific Northwest Insect Management Handbook's [mint root borer page](https://pnwhandbooks.org/insect/agronomic/mint/mint-mint-root-borer); for other insects of mint in Wisconsin, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/). UW Vegetable Entomology's [Pests by Crop](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/crops/) covers mint growing in Wisconsin, and its [Predictive Modeling](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/ipm/predictive-modeling/) page lists the mint root borer's degree-day settings.

## AgWeather's models

The [mint root borer model](/models/mint-root-borer) counts degree days from April 1 at a base of 50 °F with no upper limit, and rates risk from 0 to 4 in one window: from the first moths at 430 degree days, through a peak at the peak of egg hatch, 1,280, to an end at 2,150. Those three numbers are events in Oregon State's model, which was developed for the Pacific Northwest and is listed as partly validated; the end is when half the larvae have formed their winter chambers, not when all have.[^uspest-mrb][^agweather-mint-root-borer] Its April 1 start is Oregon State's too.[^uspest-mrb] The model comes from VDIFN, the forecasting tool AgWeather replaces, and gives the same ratings as VDIFN for the same degree days. It hasn't been compared with trap catches or larval counts in Wisconsin.[^agweather-mint-root-borer]

We worked out what the window means on the calendar from AgWeather's long-term averages (degree days for 1996 to 2025): in an average year at Arlington it opens about June 4, peaks about July 18 and ends about August 29; at Rhinelander it opens about June 13, peaks in early August, and the season's warmth doesn't reach 2,150. The risk shown covers moth flight, egg laying and the larvae's feeding until they settle for the winter. Use it to time moth trapping and plan control, and count larvae in the soil after harvest to decide whether to treat.
