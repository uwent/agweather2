The imported cabbageworm (_Pieris rapae_) is the caterpillar of the cabbage white butterfly, and the most important insect pest of cole crops in Wisconsin. It feeds on cabbage, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cauliflower, rutabaga and the other cole crops.[^uw-hort] With the [cabbage looper](/topics/cabbage-looper) and the diamondback moth, it makes up the caterpillar complex that growers scout for together.[^uw-vegento][^a3422-2026] It came to North America from Europe in the mid-1800s and has spread across the continent.[^umn-vegedge]

## Identification

- **Adults** are the white butterflies seen in numbers on warm summer days, with black wing tips. Females have two black spots on each forewing, the smaller males one.[^uw-hort][^umn-vegedge]
- **Eggs** are yellow, ridged and cone-shaped, laid one at a time, mostly on leaves.[^uw-vegento][^umn-vegedge]
- **Larvae** are velvety green caterpillars up to 1 inch long with a faint yellow stripe down the back. They lie along leaf veins and blend in well.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento]

Behavior helps tell the three caterpillars apart: an imported cabbageworm usually stays still when disturbed, a diamondback moth larva wriggles, and a cabbage looper inches away or thrashes its head.[^uw-vegento]

## Life cycle

Imported cabbageworms spend the winter as pupae (chrysalises) on plant debris. Butterflies emerge in early May and lay eggs, which hatch in about a week.[^uw-vegento][^uw-hort] The first generation feeds on cruciferous weeds and early-planted cole crops. Caterpillars feed for about 11 to 20 days, and adults emerge 6 to 11 days after pupating.[^uw-vegento]

Butterflies of the second generation appear in mid-July. Nearly all of their caterpillars grow up on planted cole crops, and this generation does the most damage.[^uw-hort] Counts of later generations vary: UW–Madison's fact sheet says there are usually three in a season, UW Vegetable Entomology three to six, and the University of Minnesota three to five, overlapping.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento][^umn-vegedge] Of the three caterpillars, the diamondback moth tends to appear first, then the imported cabbageworm, then the cabbage looper, which migrates from the south.[^mwvg-2026]

## Damage

Caterpillars chew large, irregular holes between the large veins, and older larvae working toward the center of the plant can leave only the main veins. Heavy feeding stunts cabbage and cauliflower heads, and damage to the bud of young cabbage can keep a head from forming.[^uw-hort] Large amounts of greenish-brown droppings (frass) contaminate heads and leaves and lower their market quality.[^uw-hort] Young seedlings and transplants are most easily hurt; older plants tolerate some leaf loss.[^umn-caterpillars]

## Monitoring

- Scout weekly through the season. Walk a W-shaped path and examine 25 to 50 plants. Count a plant as infested if it has eggs or caterpillars of any of the three species.[^a3422-2026]
- Look on lower leaves and leaf undersides as well as the heads. Fresh frass is a sign caterpillars are present, but rely on caterpillar counts.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento]
- Treat when the share of infested plants passes the threshold for the crop's stage. For cabbage: 10 % in the seedbed, 30 % from transplanting to cupping, 20 % from cupping to early head and 10 % at mature head. For broccoli and cauliflower: 10 % in the seedbed, 50 % from transplanting to the first flower bud or curd, and 10 % after.[^a3422-2026]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Clean transplants:** start with transplants free of caterpillars and eggs.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento]
- **Sanitation:** turn under crop residue after harvest, rotate cole crops between fields, and remove mustard-family weeds, which feed caterpillars before the crop is planted.[^uw-vegento][^umn-caterpillars]
- **Natural enemies:** predators such as paper wasps and parasitic wasps and flies often keep numbers down. The parasitic wasp _Cotesia glomerata_ typically parasitizes 20 to 30 % of caterpillars in south-central Minnesota. Broad-spectrum insecticides kill these allies.[^umn-caterpillars][^umn-vegedge][^uw-hort]
- **Row covers or netting** keep butterflies from laying eggs and suit small plantings; in gardens, caterpillars can also be picked off by hand.[^uw-hort][^umn-caterpillars]

**Insecticides** work best on small caterpillars, with good coverage of the leaves.[^uw-hort] Selective products spare natural enemies: _Bacillus thuringiensis_ (Bt; IRAC group 11A) subspecies _kurstaki_ or _aizawai_, which caterpillars must eat, spinosyns (group 5; spinosad, which is listed for organic production, and spinetoram) and insect growth regulators (group 18; methoxyfenozide, tebufenozide).[^a3422-2026][^mwvg-2026][^uw-vegento] Other classes listed for the caterpillar complex include diamides (group 28; chlorantraniliprole, cyantraniliprole), avermectins (group 6; emamectin benzoate), indoxacarb (group 22A) and pyrethroids (group 3A), which also kill natural enemies.[^a3422-2026][^umn-caterpillars][^irac-moa] Resistance is a concern with all caterpillars on cole crops, most of all the diamondback moth, so rotate classes.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [imported cabbageworm page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/imported-cabbageworm/) and the University of Minnesota's [VegEdge imported cabbageworm page](https://vegedge.umn.edu/node/16) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see UW–Madison's [imported cabbageworm fact sheet](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/imported-cabbageworm).

## AgWeather's models

The [imported cabbageworm model](/models/imported-cabbageworm) counts degree days from January 1 by the modified average method, base 50 °F with a cutoff at 86 °F, as UW–Madison Extension's guide to calculating degree days describes. UW–Madison's degree-day table puts the spring butterflies at 150 to 240 degree days;[^uw-degree-days] in the model a tenth of the spring flight has emerged by 150 and half by 240. Eggs and caterpillars follow each flight, and the model's risk is the caterpillars feeding. Later generations follow about every 840 degree days until fall freezes end the season, and a window marks when each generation's eggs are hatching, the time to scout for eggs and young caterpillars.[^agweather-imported-cabbageworm]

We checked the spring flight against iNaturalist records of cabbage white butterflies in Wisconsin from 2016 to 2026, corrected for how many people were looking: the middle of the spring flight came at 234 degree days, against the model's 239. The time between later flights was set from the same records, so the model's agreement with them there isn't an independent test. On AgWeather's long-term averages (degree days for 1996 to 2025), the spring flight begins in late April or early May, close to the early May that UW–Madison gives, and the second flight comes in early July in southern Wisconsin, a week or two before the mid-July that UW–Madison gives.[^agweather-imported-cabbageworm][^uw-hort] The model weighs the second generation most in its risk, since it does the most damage, and it hasn't been compared with counts of caterpillars. Use it as a reminder of when to scout, and base sprays on your own counts and the thresholds above.
