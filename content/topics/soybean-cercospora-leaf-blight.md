Cercospora leaf blight is a late-season disease of soybean leaves, leaf stalks (petioles), stems and pods, caused by several species of _Cercospora_ fungi. The disease was long blamed on _C. kikuchii_, but a survey across the South, from Texas to Alabama and north to Missouri and Kentucky, found _C. flagellaris_ the main species. Others, such as _C. sigesbeckiae_, also occur in the United States. Which species matter most farther north isn't well known.[^price-2025] The same fungi cause purple seed stain. The disease is most common in the South, but it also occurs in the northern Midwest. UW–Madison's field crops guide notes that it is becoming more common in Wisconsin, and the state's field crops pathologist has found it in fields around the state.[^malvick-clb][^smith-2014][^a3646-2025]

## Identification

- **When and where:** symptoms usually appear from beginning seed (R5) on, on the upper leaves exposed to the sun.[^price-2025][^cpn-clb]
- **On leaves:** the first signs are small purplish specks or light purple patches on the upper surface. The upper canopy then turns bronze to purple, and the leaves feel leathery and look sunburned. Brown dead areas can follow, and the upper leaves may drop.[^price-2025][^malvick-clb]
- **On petioles, stems and pods:** purple to reddish-purple lesions form on the leaf stalks. In severe cases, or on very susceptible varieties, the main stem and pods can turn dark purple.[^price-2025][^malvick-clb]
- **On seed:** purple seed stain shows as pink to deep purple patches on the seed coat. Leaf blight in a field doesn't reliably predict purple seed stain, or the reverse.[^malvick-clb][^price-2025][^cpn-clb]
- **Look-alikes:** sunburn causes brick-red color between the veins, mostly on the underside of the leaf, where leaf blight is mostly on the upper side. Chloride toxicity follows the leaf edges. Frogeye leaf spot makes gray spots with reddish-brown borders, and its seed stain is light purple to gray rather than deep purple.[^price-2025]

## Life cycle

The fungi survive the winter on crop residue and in infected seed, which can bring the disease into a field at planting. Rain and wind spread spores within the crop. Plants can be infected from flowering until maturity, though symptoms show late. Warm, humid weather with long dew periods favors the disease.[^price-2025][^malvick-clb]

Late-planted soybeans and later-maturing susceptible varieties are at higher risk. Besides soybean, the fungi can infect corn, cotton, pokeweed and ragweed.[^price-2025]

## Damage

Losses range from mild to severe. In bad years leaf blight can strip the upper canopy, though complete defoliation is rare, and purple seed stain lowers seed quality. Severe losses are mainly a problem in the South.[^price-2025][^malvick-clb]

## Monitoring

- Scout the upper canopy from beginning pod through seed fill, and watch for the purple and bronze color on leaves in full sun.[^price-2025]
- Rule out sunburn, chloride toxicity and frogeye leaf spot before deciding on a fungicide. If you aren't sure, send a sample to a plant disease lab.[^price-2025]
- At harvest, note purple seed stain in each variety, and keep stained seed out of next year's planting.[^malvick-clb]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls** come first:

- **Choose varieties with low susceptibility**, using ratings from trials near your farm.[^price-2025][^malvick-clb]
- **Plant clean seed.**[^malvick-clb]
- **Rotate out of soybeans, or till,** so residue breaks down before the next soybean crop.[^price-2025][^a3646-2025]

**Fungicides** from more than one group, applied at the right stage, are the main chemical control:

- **QoI fungicides (strobilurins, FRAC group 11) are likely no longer effective:** resistance to them has been confirmed in eight states.[^price-2025]
- **Some mixtures of an SDHI (group 7) and a DMI (group 3),** applied from beginning pod (R3) to beginning seed (R5), remain effective.[^price-2025]
- In UW–Madison's ratings, no fungicide rates better than fair to good against Cercospora leaf blight.[^a3646-2025]

For products and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf). The Crop Protection Network's [An Overview of Cercospora Leaf Blight](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/publications/an-overview-of-cercospora-leaf-blight-of-soybean) and Minnesota's [Cercospora leaf blight and purple seed stain](https://extension.umn.edu/agriculture/crop-production/soybean/cercospora-leaf-blight-and-purple-seed-stain-on-soybean) explain the choices in more detail.

## AgWeather's models

The [soybean Cercospora model](/models/soybean-cercospora) estimates the probability of spores for three species from recent weather: _C. flagellaris_ from the minimum temperature and the gap between temperature and dew point over 5 days; _C. sigesbeckiae_ from the highest wind speeds over 14 days and the humid hours over 10 days; and _C. kikuchii_ from the humid hours over 14 days and the largest gap between temperature and dew point over 5 days. The risk shown is the highest of the three.[^agweather-soybean-cercospora]

The model is a beta version, and its limits are large. We copied it from the Crop Protection Network's Crop Risk Tool, and it matches the tool exactly, but the equations are Wade Webster's (North Dakota State University) and haven't been published. We don't know where, in which years or on what weather they were fitted, whether they predict spores caught in traps or disease in fields, or which crop stages they apply to. We show the risk from beginning pod (R3) to full seed (R6) as our own provisional choice. Nor do we know whether the _C. sigesbeckiae_ equation was fitted to wind in kilometers per hour or in meters per second. We follow the tool, which uses kilometers per hour; read the other way, the same weather can give that species a probability of 86 % instead of 6 %. The model hasn't been compared with Cercospora leaf blight in fields, so treat it as a rough guide.[^agweather-soybean-cercospora]
