Cercospora leaf spot is a disease of beet leaves caused by the fungus _Cercospora beticola_. It attacks table beet, sugar beet, Swiss chard and spinach. It is the most destructive leaf disease of table beet in Wisconsin, and the most damaging leaf disease of sugar beet in Minnesota and North Dakota.[^cornell-beet-2021][^dixon-2023][^ndsu-cls]

## Identification

- **Spots** are round, about ⅛ inch across, with ash-gray to tan centers. On red beets and sugar beet the border is reddish purple to dark brown; on yellow beets it is tan to brown.[^cornell-beet-2021][^ndsu-cls]
- **With a hand lens** you can see tiny black dots in the centers of the spots, the structures that bear the spores. In humid weather the spores make spots look gray and fuzzy.[^cornell-beet-2021][^harveson-2013][^ndsu-cls]
- **Spots appear first on the older, lower leaves** and move up to younger ones. They run together, and leaves yellow and die but stay attached; a badly diseased field looks scorched.[^harveson-2013][^khan-2023]
- **Bacterial leaf spot** can look similar; NDSU's [comparison](https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/publications/comparison-cercospora-and-bacterial-leaf-spots-sugarbeet) shows the differences.[^khan-2023]

## Life cycle

The fungus spends the winter on infested leaf debris from the last beet crop and can survive in the soil on that debris for up to 3 years. It also persists on weeds such as lambsquarters and pigweed, and on seed.[^cornell-beet-2021][^khan-2023] Wind, splashing rain, insects, workers and equipment spread the spores.[^cornell-beet-2021]

Infection needs a warm, wet day: leaves wet (or humidity above 90 %) for more than 11 hours, at temperatures above 60 °F. Days of 80 to 90 °F with nights above 60 °F favor it most.[^harveson-2013][^ndsu-cls] Spots appear 5 to 21 days after infection, and in midsummer the fungus can complete a cycle in about 10 days, so it builds through many cycles in a season.[^khan-2023][^harveson-2013] Warm, wet weather favors it most from midseason on.[^cornell-beet-2021]

## Damage

- **Sugar beet:** losses can approach 40 %, through both lower root yield and lower sugar content. Low-sugar beets also store poorly.[^harveson-2013][^ndsu-cls]
- **Table beet:** heavy leaf loss reduces yield and can disrupt harvesting with machines that lift beets by their tops. Buyers may turn away bunched beets sold with their tops once spots cover more than 5 % of the leaf area.[^dixon-2023][^cornell-beet-2021]

## Monitoring

- In sugar beet, scout first the fields most at risk: susceptible cultivars, closed rows, and fields beside shelterbelts, waterways or last year's beet fields.[^ndsu-cls]
- Spots you see now come from infections 7 to 10 days earlier, so more is under way than shows.[^harveson-2013]
- Follow daily infection values (DIVs), developed for sugar beet, which score each day from 0 to 7 by its hours of high humidity and their temperature. A two-day total of 7 or more means a strong chance of infection; below 6, little.[^harveson-2013][^ndawn-cls] The [North Dakota Agricultural Weather Network (NDAWN)](https://ndawn.ndsu.nodak.edu/help-sugarbeet-cercospora.html) posts DIVs for its stations, and the sugar beet factory districts of Minnesota and North Dakota issue daily spray advisories from them.[^ndawn-cls]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- Rotate away from beets, Swiss chard and spinach for at least 3 years, and bury beet debris by tillage after harvest.[^cornell-beet-2021][^ndsu-cls]
- Plant as far as you can from last year's infected fields; NDSU suggests at least 100 yards from last year's infected sugar beet fields.[^khan-2023]
- Control lambsquarters and pigweed, use certified, treated seed, and avoid overhead irrigation or irrigate at midday so leaves dry.[^cornell-beet-2021]
- Choose resistant cultivars. Sugar beet cultivars with improved resistance are available and are the most effective tool when combined with timely sprays. Table beet cultivars in Wisconsin trials varied widely, but all were susceptible compared with resistant sugar beets.[^ndsu-cls][^dixon-2023]

**Fungicides:** in sugar beet, no single fungicide controls the disease all season; start after the rows close, at the first spots in the field or district.[^ndsu-cls]

- **Use mixtures and rotate.** In sugar beet, mixtures that include a multisite fungicide, used in a rotation, are most effective. _C. beticola_ has become resistant or less sensitive to most fungicides used against it in Minnesota and North Dakota, and most New York populations resist quinone outside inhibitors (QoIs, FRAC group 11).[^ndsu-cls][^cornell-beet-2021]
- **Classes:** demethylation inhibitors (DMIs, group 3; propiconazole, tebuconazole), succinate dehydrogenase inhibitors (SDHIs, group 7; penthiopyrad, fluxapyroxad, pydiflumetofen), QoIs (group 11; azoxystrobin, pyraclostrobin, trifloxystrobin) and copper (group M01).[^a3422-2026][^cornell-beet-2021]
- **Organic options** are limited: two OMRI-listed treatments failed in Wisconsin trials, while in New York copper octanoate (group M01) tank-mixed with a _Bacillus_ biofungicide (group BM02) beat either alone.[^dixon-2023][^cornell-beet-2021][^frac-2026]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide); sugar beet growers should also follow their cooperative's agronomists. Cornell's [table beet fact sheet](https://www.vegetables.cornell.edu/pest-management/disease-factsheets/cercospora-leaf-spot-of-table-beet/) and NDSU's [sugar beet page](https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/ag-hub/ag-topics/crop-production/crops/sugarbeets/cercospora-leaf-spot-sugarbeet) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see the University of Minnesota's [beet leaf spot guide](https://apps.extension.umn.edu/garden/diagnose/plant/vegetable/beet/leavesspots.html).

## AgWeather's models

The [Cercospora leaf spot model](/models/cercospora-beet-div) gives each day 0 to 7 DIVs from the hours of high humidity and their mean temperature, from the table that Windels and colleagues described for the sugar beet industry in Minnesota and North Dakota; we haven't yet checked our copy of the table against their paper. It rates risk from the higher of the last 2 days' and last 7 days' average: High begins at a 2-day average of 3.5 (the same as a two-day total of 7) or a 7-day average of 3. NDAWN counts hours at 85 % humidity or more from noon to noon, adjusted to the crop canopy, so its values can differ from ours. We take high humidity as a stand-in for leaf wetness. The model was developed for sugar beet in the Red River Valley, and we haven't yet compared it with Cercospora leaf spot in Wisconsin table beet fields.[^agweather-cercospora-beet-div][^windels-1998][^ndawn-cls]
