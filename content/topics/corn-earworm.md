The corn earworm (_Helicoverpa zea_) is a moth whose caterpillars feed in the tips of corn ears. It does the most harm in fresh-market and processing sweet corn and in seed corn. On tomatoes it is called the tomato fruitworm, and it also feeds on peppers, snap beans and lettuce.[^uw-vegento] Most moths fly in from the south each summer, so the pressure changes a great deal from year to year.[^uw-vegento][^umn-cew]

## Identification

- **Adults** are stout, grayish-brown moths with a wingspan of about 1½ inches. The front wings have dark, irregular lines and a dark patch near the tip.[^uw-vegento]
- **Eggs** are tiny (1/32 inch), ridged, flattened spheres laid one at a time on fresh silks. They are light yellow at first and darken to brown before they hatch.[^uw-vegento][^uw-hort]
- **Caterpillars** grow to about 2 inches and vary in color from green, tan and pink to maroon or black, with dark stripes along the back and a plain yellowish head.[^uw-vegento][^uw-hort] Unlike western bean cutworm caterpillars, which also feed in ears, they have dark spines and bumps and stripes along the sides.[^a3646-2025]

## Life cycle

Corn earworms spend the winter as pupae in the soil in the southern states; pupae rarely survive Wisconsin winters.[^uw-hort] Moths ride weather systems north. A small flight usually arrives in June and threatens mainly very early sweet corn. Larger flights follow from late July, and moths arriving from mid-August to early September do the most damage.[^umn-cew][^uw-hort][^uw-vegento] How many arrive depends on winds from the Gulf Coast and on numbers in the South.[^umn-cew]

Moths fly mostly at night. Females lay up to 1,000 eggs each, singly on fresh silks, and prefer sweet corn from late tassel to early silk.[^uw-vegento][^umn-cew] Eggs hatch in 2 to 6 days, or within a day in hot weather, and the young caterpillars crawl straight down the silk channel to the ear tip.[^umn-cew][^uw-vegento] They are cannibals, so usually only one is found in each ear. After two to three weeks of feeding they drop to the ground and pupate in the soil. Egg to adult takes about 30 days in midsummer.[^uw-vegento][^umn-cew]

## Damage

- **Sweet corn:** caterpillars eat kernels at the ear tip and leave droppings (frass). A grown caterpillar eats 8 to 15 kernels, or the top 1 to 2 inches of the ear, and its holes let in fungi.[^umn-cew] Even heavy infestations damage under 10 % of the kernels, but shoppers reject damaged fresh-market ears, and processors must cut off the tips.[^uw-vegento]
- **Seed corn:** the crop's high value makes even small losses costly. In ordinary field corn the loss rarely justifies control.[^uw-vegento]
- **Tomatoes and snap beans** can be damaged late in the season, when corn is less attractive.[^uw-hort]

## Monitoring

- **Pheromone traps** are the best way to know when moths are present. Hang one 4 to 6 feet above the ground on the south or west side of a field by green silk. Change lures every two weeks, keep spare lures frozen, and take used lures away from the trap. Black light traps catch fewer earworm moths.[^uw-vegento]
- **Thresholds:** UW–Madison suggests treating sweet corn from first silk until the silks brown when pheromone traps catch 5 to 10 moths a night, or black light traps 3 to 5, for three nights in a row. Check silks for eggs to confirm.[^uw-vegento] For seed corn, UW–Madison's field crop guide suggests treating at 10 % silk at 5 to 10 moths a night.[^a3646-2025] Thresholds differ from state to state and depend on the type of trap used.[^owens-2023]
- DATCP posts weekly [corn earworm trap counts](https://datcp.wi.gov/Pages/Programs_Services/CornEarwormTraps.aspx) from sites across Wisconsin from July through September.[^datcp-cew]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Planting dates:** mid-season varieties that mature between the June and late-summer flights escape the worst of the damage.[^uw-hort]
- **Natural enemies:** _Trichogramma_ and other small wasps parasitize eggs, and minute pirate bugs eat them. Buying and releasing them costs too much to pay, but spraying only when needed and avoiding broad-spectrum products conserves the ones already there.[^uw-vegento]
- **Bt sweet corn** gives a high level of control.[^umn-cew] Corn earworm has become resistant to most Bt proteins, though: only Vip3A has no widespread documented resistance.[^iowa-cew][^owens-2023]
- **Silk treatments:** _Bacillus thuringiensis_ (Bt; IRAC group 11A) in vegetable oil, applied to each ear's silks with a hand applicator, can give 80 to 90 % control when well timed and is used in organic production.[^uw-vegento][^irac-moa] In home gardens, about 20 drops of mineral oil (group UNM, which acts physically) on the silks inside the ear tip, 3 to 7 days after silks appear, helps protect the ear.[^uw-hort]

**Insecticides** must be on the silks when eggs hatch, because caterpillars inside the ear are out of reach. On sweet corn, spray every 3 to 4 days from first silk until the silks brown, with nozzles aimed at the ear zone.[^uw-vegento] Stop when 90 % of silks are brown.[^iowa-2024] Four classes are labeled for silking sweet corn: pyrethroids (IRAC group 3A), spinosyns (group 5; spinosad and spinetoram), diamides (group 28; chlorantraniliprole) and carbamates (group 1A; methomyl).[^owens-2023] Pyrethroid-resistant earworms are well documented in the South and mid-Atlantic and have been found in Iowa, so don't rely on pyrethroids alone, and rotate classes.[^iowa-2024][^owens-2023]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [corn earworm page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/corn-earworm/) and Iowa State's [corn earworm guidelines](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/pests/insects/earworm) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see UW–Madison's [corn earworm fact sheet](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/corn-earworm-helicoverpa-zea/).

## AgWeather's models

The [corn earworm model](/models/corn-earworm) counts degree days at a base of 55 °F with an upper limit of 92 °F from August 1, and rates risk from 0 to 4 from 70 degree days, when eggs laid on that date hatch, to 440, when the caterpillars leave the ears, peaking at 275. It comes from VDIFN, the forecasting tool AgWeather replaces, and gives the same ratings as VDIFN for the same degree days. The study its numbers come from hasn't yet been read, and the model hasn't been compared with Wisconsin's trap counts.[^agweather-cew]

August 1 stands in for the date you first find eggs on silks, which UW Vegetable Entomology says is typically around then.[^uw-vegento] Because the moths' arrival changes from year to year, a fixed date can be weeks off, and counting from August 1 misses the June flight that reaches early sweet corn. When you look up a single location, you can change the model's start date (its biofix) to the day you first find eggs on silks or first catch moths in your traps; maps count from August 1. Either way, keep timing sprays by your traps.
