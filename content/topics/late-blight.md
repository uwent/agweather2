Late blight is a disease of potato and tomato caused by _Phytophthora infestans_, a water mold rather than a true fungus. It also infects other plants in the nightshade family, such as pepper, petunia and nightshade weeds.[^vegpath-potato][^gevens-2024] It appears in Wisconsin in some years and not others, when cool, wet weather and a source of spores come together, and in those years it can destroy a planting within days.[^a3422-2026][^umass-2017] It is a regulated disease in Wisconsin.[^a3422-2026]

## Identification

- **On leaves**, pale or olive-green patches grow quickly, often past the size of a nickel, and turn brown to black, with no clear edge. In humid weather a white, fuzzy growth forms on the spots, mostly on the underside of the leaf.[^gevens-2024][^umass-2017][^johnson-2020][^vegpath-tomato]
- **On stems**, lesions are brown to black. **On tomato fruit**, spots are firm, often sunken, and golden to chocolate brown, sometimes with rings.[^gevens-2024][^vegpath-tomato]
- **On potato tubers**, reddish-brown to purple, sunken patches cover a firm brown rot that can reach an inch or more into the flesh.[^gevens-2024][^johnson-2020][^vegpath-potato]

## Life cycle

The pathogen needs living plant tissue and is unlikely to overwinter in Wisconsin soil. It carries over in infected potato tubers (seed, cull piles and tubers left in the field that sprout as volunteers) and in plant material kept warm over winter, such as tomatoes in a compost pile. It can also arrive on infected transplants, or as spores blown in on the wind from infected fields elsewhere.[^gevens-2024][^vegpath-potato][^umass-2017]

Spots produce spores in very humid air (90 to 100 percent relative humidity) at about 54 to 75 °F. On a wet leaf, spores can infect from 46 to 75 °F, in as little as 2 hours in ideal weather.[^vegpath-potato] New spots appear about 7 days later and produce spores of their own, so the disease builds quickly. Spores die within a few hours in dry, sunny weather, and hot, dry summers slow the disease.[^johnson-2020][^umass-2017]

Several strains have been found in Wisconsin, and some are not controlled by mefenoxam, so the strain in an outbreak matters for choosing fungicides.[^a3422-2026]

## Damage

In cool, wet weather, whole plants can collapse and die within 7 to 10 days.[^gevens-2024] On potato, spores washed from the leaves into the soil infect tubers, and tubers can also be infected at harvest, especially where the skin is scraped.[^johnson-2020] On tomato, fruit with symptoms should not be eaten or preserved, but healthy-looking fruit from infected plants is safe.[^gevens-2024]

## Monitoring

- Scout plantings regularly, daily if you can.[^umass-2017]
- Follow disease severity values, counted from crop emergence or your last fungicide spray, whichever is more recent.[^vegpath-potato] Disease is expected 7 to 10 days after the total reaches 18.[^umass-2017] UW Vegetable Pathology treats a total of 18 as the threshold for a first preventive fungicide spray, and Wisconsin's commercial growers use forecasts built on these values to time their sprays.[^vcu-2025][^vegpath-tomato]
- Watch for confirmed reports nearby. During the season, UW Vegetable Pathology's weekly Vegetable Crop Update gives disease severity value totals across Wisconsin and news of confirmed late blight finds and their strains.[^vcu-2025]
- Have suspect plants confirmed by the UW Plant Disease Diagnostic Clinic or UW Vegetable Pathology, which also identifies the strain of samples from potato fields.[^vegpath-potato][^a3422-2026]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls** remove the plants the pathogen survives in and slow its spread:

- Plant certified seed potatoes each year, and don't save your own tubers for seed.[^gevens-2024][^johnson-2020]
- Destroy cull piles, volunteer potatoes and tomatoes, and nightshade weeds.[^vegpath-potato][^johnson-2020]
- Start with healthy tomato transplants, and grow resistant varieties; resistance varies with the strain, so several varieties improve the odds.[^umass-2017][^gevens-2024]
- Space rows and plants so the canopy dries.[^vegpath-potato]
- Destroy an infected spot along with a border of healthy-looking plants. In gardens, bag infected plants for the trash, never the compost.[^umass-2017][^gevens-2024]
- Kill potato vines at least 2 weeks, and preferably 3, before harvest, so the skins set and fewer live spores remain in the soil.[^johnson-2020]

**Fungicides** work only if they are on the plant before infection, and they won't control late blight once it is widespread in a field.[^vegpath-tomato][^johnson-2020]

- **Protectants** are the base of a program: chlorothalonil (FRAC group M5), mancozeb (group M3) and fixed copper. They also control early blight.[^a3422-2026] Include one in every spray, and shorten the interval when disease pressure is high.[^umass-2017]
- **Fungicides aimed at water molds** include cyazofamid (group 21), mandipropamid and dimethomorph (group 40), oxathiapiprolin (group 49), fluazinam (group 29), propamocarb (group 28) and cymoxanil (group 27). Tank-mix them with a protectant and rotate groups.[^a3422-2026][^fronk-2026] Mefenoxam (group 4) controls only some strains.[^a3422-2026]
- **Organic growers** rely mainly on copper, and home gardeners can use chlorothalonil or copper products labeled for tomato or potato.[^fronk-2026][^gevens-2024]

For products and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/). UW Vegetable Pathology's [potato](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/diseases/potato-late-blight/) and [tomato](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/diseases/tomato-late-blight) late blight pages and the University of Maine's [potato late blight bulletin](https://extension.umaine.edu/publications/2441e/) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see UW–Madison's [late blight fact sheet](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/late-blight/).

## AgWeather's models

The [late blight model](/models/late-blight-dsv) gives each day a disease severity value from the hours of high humidity and their temperature, and adds them up from crop emergence or your last fungicide spray. A season total of 18 is the usual threshold for a first spray, and the risk rises further with high totals over the last 14 days. Humid hours stand in for leaf wetness, so irrigated fields may stay wet longer than the model shows. The model reproduces the Crop Risk Tool it replaces, but it hasn't yet been compared with late blight outbreaks in Wisconsin.
