Frogeye leaf spot is a leaf disease of soybean caused by the fungus _Cercospora sojina_. It occurs across the United States and in Ontario, Canada. Long a disease of the South, it has been reported as far north as southern Wisconsin, and UW–Madison's field crops guide notes that it is becoming more common in the state. Hot, humid weather favors it.[^bradley-2016][^malvick-2018][^a3646-2025]

## Identification

- **On leaves:** the first spots are tiny, dark and water-soaked, on the upper side of the leaf. As they grow, round or angular, their centers fade from gray through brown to light tan, ringed by a thin reddish-purple edge: the "frog eye." Some varieties show a light green halo around each spot. In favorable weather the underside of the spot turns gray and fuzzy as the fungus makes spores.[^bradley-2016]
- **Old spots:** the centers may turn white or fall out, leaving a shot-hole look.[^malvick-2018]
- **Where:** young leaves are the most susceptible, so spots usually show in the upper canopy.[^bradley-2016]
- **Stems, pods and seed:** stem lesions are long and hard to recognize late in the season; pod lesions are oblong and look like the leaf spots. Infected seed can turn light purple to gray, or show nothing.[^bradley-2016]
- **Look-alikes:** Phyllosticta leaf spot and injury from PPO-inhibiting herbicides (WSSA group 14) or paraquat (group 22) can look similar. If you aren't sure, send a sample to a plant disease lab.[^stoetzer-2018][^wssa-hrac][^take-action-herbicides]

## Life cycle

The fungus survives the winter in soybean residue, where it can last at least two years, and in seed, though spread by seed is rarely seen in the field. Wind and splashing rain carry its spores to soybean leaves, within a field and to neighboring fields.[^bradley-2016]

Infection can happen at any stage but is most common after flowering, and symptoms are usually seen from beginning bloom (R1) to beginning maturity (R7). Spots take about two weeks to appear after infection, so they don't show on young, expanding leaves even though those are infected most easily.[^bradley-2016][^cpn-frogeye] The disease is favored by warm, humid weather with frequent rain over a long stretch, and by several overcast days in a row.[^bradley-2016]

The risk is highest in fields with a history of the disease, in continuous soybean or short rotations, in conservation tillage and with susceptible varieties.[^stoetzer-2018]

## Damage

Severe frogeye leaf spot can strip leaves early. Yield losses can reach 35 % when a severe outbreak starts early or just after flowering, but they are small if the disease starts partway through seed fill (after R5.5) or stays light. Infected seed may also be discolored.[^bradley-2016]

## Monitoring

- Scout the upper canopy from flowering on, starting with susceptible varieties and fields where frogeye leaf spot has been found before.[^bradley-2016][^stoetzer-2018]
- There is no set threshold for spraying. Weigh the crop's stage, the weather, the field's history and the variety's resistance.[^stoetzer-2018]
- If you spray, scout again about two weeks later to see whether the fungicide controlled the disease. Poor control can be a sign of fungicide resistance; contact your county Extension office or a crop disease specialist.[^bradley-2016][^stoetzer-2018]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls** reduce the fungus carried from one soybean crop to the next:

- **Plant resistant varieties** where the disease is a concern. The _Rcs3_ resistance gene has worked against every race of the fungus known in North America, but some varieties sold as resistant are only partly so.[^bradley-2016][^stoetzer-2018]
- **Rotate** with corn, small grains or alfalfa, which aren't hosts. After a severe outbreak, a longer rotation may be needed.[^bradley-2016][^cpn-frogeye]
- **Bury or break down residue** by tillage where the disease was severe, and plant clean seed.[^bradley-2016][^malvick-2018]

**Fungicides** applied during pod development, at beginning pod (R3) to full pod (R4), work best. No fungicide gives complete control on a susceptible variety.[^bradley-2016][^stoetzer-2018]

- **Don't rely on QoI fungicides (strobilurins, FRAC group 11).** Strains of the fungus that resist them have been found in North America, and they can be widespread: an Iowa State survey found resistant strains in nearly every one of 73 fields across 51 Iowa counties. The resistance usually comes from a single change in the fungus, so a strain that resists one QoI most likely resists them all. UW–Madison's guide rates QoI products used alone as poor to fair against frogeye leaf spot.[^bradley-2016][^stoetzer-mueller-2020][^a3646-2025]
- **Use other groups, or mixtures that contain them.** In UW–Madison's ratings, thiophanate-methyl (FRAC group 1), prothioconazole (a DMI, group 3) and some mixtures of a DMI with a QoI, or with a QoI and an SDHI (group 7), rate very good. Rotate groups, and never rely on a single group.[^a3646-2025][^bradley-2016]

For products and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf). The Crop Protection Network's [An Overview of Frogeye Leaf Spot](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/publications/an-overview-of-frogeye-leaf-spot) and Iowa State's article on [fungicide resistance in frogeye leaf spot](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/cropnews/2020/01/instances-frogeye-leaf-spot-resistance-qois-abundant-iowa) explain the choices in more detail.

## AgWeather's models

The [frogeye leaf spot model](/models/frogeye-leaf-spot) estimates the probability that the fungus's spores are present, from 30-day averages of the daily high temperature and of the hours each day with relative humidity of 80 % or more. Its risk rises steadily with warmth and humid hours. It shows the risk only from beginning bloom (R1) to beginning seed (R5), taken from the [soybean stages model](/models/soybean-stages) or your own dates.[^agweather-frogeye]

We copied the model from the Crop Protection Network's Crop Risk Tool, and it matches the tool exactly. The tool credits the equation to Damon Smith (UW–Madison) and Richard Webster (North Dakota State University), but we haven't found it published, so we can't say where, in which years or on what weather it was fitted, or whether it predicts spores caught in traps or disease in fields. Its risk levels (Moderate from 40 %, High from 50 %) are the tool's, and we haven't compared the model with frogeye leaf spot in Wisconsin fields. Read it as a sign of when the weather favors the fungus, alongside scouting, the field's history and the variety's resistance.[^agweather-frogeye]
