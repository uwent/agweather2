Potato virus Y (PVY) is a virus of potato, tomato, pepper, tobacco and other plants in the nightshade family, spread from plant to plant by aphids. It can cut yields in heavily infected fields and susceptible varieties, and some strains cause tuber necrosis, which lowers storage quality.[^uw-vegpath-pvy] It matters most to seed potato growers: Wisconsin's seed lots are certified only if very few plants carry mosaic viruses such as PVY.[^atcp-156]

## Identification

**The aphids.** Aphids are small, soft-bodied, pear-shaped insects with a pair of short tubes (cornicles) at the rear of the abdomen. Adults may or may not have wings.[^uw-vegento-aphids] Two groups matter for PVY:

- **Colonizing aphids** feed and reproduce on potato: the green peach aphid, the potato aphid and, less often, the buckthorn aphid. Small groups of three to six wingless aphids, often with white cast skins, mean a colonizing species is getting established.[^uw-vegento-pvy] The green peach aphid, yellowish green with three dark lines on the back, is perhaps the most efficient vector of PVY and the most insecticide-resistant aphid in Wisconsin.[^cornell-vectors][^uw-vegento-aphids]
- **Non-colonizing aphids** pass through potato fields without settling, among them the soybean aphid, corn leaf aphid, bird cherry-oat aphid, English grain aphid and pea aphid.[^uw-vegento-pvy] They transmit PVY less efficiently than colonizers, but their numbers make them the main spreaders of PVY in U.S. potatoes.[^cornell-vectors]

**The virus.** Symptoms depend on the strain, the variety and the weather: mosaic patterns of yellow and green, wrinkling, dead spots or streaks, leaf drop and stunting. Necrotic strains cause raised brown rings on tubers that can sink into dead patches. Many newer strains cause only mild leaf symptoms, so lab tests are more reliable than looking.[^cornell-symptoms][^uw-vegento-pvy][^cornell-management]

## Life cycle

Most aphids spend the winter as eggs on a perennial host, fly to summer hosts in spring, and fly again in late summer to their winter hosts. In summer, colonies of wingless females grow quickly and produce winged aphids when crowded.[^uw-vegento-aphids][^cornell-vectors]

PVY clings to the aphid's mouthparts. A winged aphid landing in a field makes brief test probes into leaves, picking up or passing on the virus within seconds. It can infect several plants as it moves on, whether or not it ever feeds on potato.[^cornell-vectors]

Non-colonizing species move through seed fields at fairly predictable times: grain aphids in midsummer, soybean and pea aphids in late summer. Hay cutting, harvest and some insecticide sprays can set aphids flying.[^uw-vegento-pvy][^cornell-vectors][^uw-vegento-aphids] The [Aphid Suction Trap Network](https://suctiontrapnetwork.org/), run from the University of Illinois since 2005, catches flying aphids weekly at about 30 sites in the Midwest, several of them in Wisconsin.[^suction-traps][^uw-vegento-research][^uw-vegento-pvy]

## Damage

Infected seed pieces grow into infected plants, which aphids spread virus from. Commercial growers who plant infected seed risk lower yields and poorer storage; seed growers risk having lots downgraded or rejected.[^uw-vegpath-pvy] In Wisconsin's post-harvest test, foundation seed may have no more than 0.5 % of plants with mosaic viruses, and other certified seed no more than 5 %.[^atcp-156] Infection late in the season can go unseen in the field and still show up in the harvested seed.[^cornell-management]

## Monitoring

- Each week, check both sides of a whole leaf from mid-canopy on 25 plants at 10 places in each field, looking hardest along southern and western edges, tree lines and drive rows, where migrating aphids tend to land. Note whether the aphids have wings: small groups of wingless aphids mean a colonizing species is settling in.[^uw-vegento-pvy]
- Wisconsin's thresholds for green peach aphid are more than 10 per 100 leaves in seed fields and 30 in table stock or processing potatoes.[^uw-vegento-aphids]
- Leaf checks miss most non-colonizing aphids, which don't stay. Yellow pan traps at field edges, the suction traps and a degree-day risk model follow their flights instead.[^uw-vegento-aphids][^uw-vegento-pvy]

## Management

**Limit sources of virus.** Planting clean seed is the best defense: don't replant seed with any measurable PVY, and judge lots by laboratory tests rather than by eye. Destroy cull potatoes and volunteers, control weed hosts such as nightshades, and clean cutting and planting equipment between seed lots.[^uw-vegento-pvy][^cornell-management]

**Plan the field.** Keep seed fields away from commercial potatoes and other nightshade crops. Borders of a crop PVY can't infect, such as rye grass, around small, early-generation lots can strip virus from aphids' mouthparts before they reach the potatoes.[^cornell-management][^uw-vegento-pvy]

**Mineral oils** (paraffinic oils) discourage aphids from probing and are the main tool against non-colonizing aphids. UW–Madison suggests starting about two weeks after full emergence and spraying weekly through the season. Apply after irrigation, not before, and in the evening if possible to limit leaf injury. Oils must cover new growth and both leaf surfaces to work.[^uw-vegento-pvy][^cornell-management]

**Insecticides** don't stop a winged aphid from passing on PVY: it does so in seconds, before any product acts, and some insecticides make aphids probe more.[^uw-vegento-aphids][^cornell-vectors] Systemic insecticides at planting protect early-generation seed from colonizing aphids but wear off by midseason.[^uw-vegento-pvy] After that, once scouting finds small colonies of wingless aphids, systemic foliar insecticides slow spread within the field. Effective groups include neonicotinoids (IRAC group 4A), sulfoxaflor (4C), flupyradifurone (4D), pymetrozine (9B), afidopyropen (9D), flonicamid (29), spirotetramat (23) and cyantraniliprole (28). Make no more than two applications in a row of one group before rotating. Green peach and melon aphids resist several insecticides, notably organophosphates (group 1B).[^uw-vegento-pvy][^uw-vegento-aphids][^irac-moa]

**Vine kill.** Plant and kill vines early to escape late-season flights. The risk of spread lasts until vine kill.[^cornell-management][^uw-vegento-pvy]

For products and current registrations in Wisconsin, see [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/) (A3422); in neighboring states, the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide).

## AgWeather's models

The [aphid PVY vectors model](/models/aphid-pvy-vectors) shows how much PVY-spreading aphid flight a typical season has at each point, from degree days at a base of 39.2 °F with an 86 °F cutoff, counted from January 1. Its curve comes from UW–Madison Vegetable Entomology's analysis of Wisconsin's suction trap catches from 2016 to 2023, with each species counted in proportion to how well it transmits PVY. Risk rises through midsummer to a broad peak near 3,490 degree days. You can also read the curve by calendar date, as a typical Wisconsin season: risk climbs from late July and peaks about August 22.[^agweather-pvy]

In AgWeather's long-term averages for 1996–2025, risk is Moderate from about June 1 in southern Wisconsin and mid-June in the north. It turns High (half the peak) around mid-July in the south and about August 2 near Antigo, in the north-central seed-growing area, and peaks in late August at Arlington and Hancock and in late September around Antigo and Rhinelander. In the north, then, risk keeps rising through most of the season.

Use the model alongside the season-long oil program, not as the signal to start it: as risk rises toward High, consider spraying oils and aphicides more often and keeping coverage up. Because risk climbs late in the season, killing vines early, before the late surge of aphids, helps keep PVY out of a seed lot.[^cornell-management][^uw-vegento-pvy]

Read it as typical pressure for the time of the season, not a forecast of this year's peak. Years differ widely: in the trap data, a year's peak came anywhere from about 1,700 to 4,800 degree days. Tested on years left out of the fit, degree days timed the start of the season's catch a little better than the calendar did, but the middle and late flight no better, because most of these aphids fly in from elsewhere. Current trap catches are a better guide when you have them. An earlier analysis of the same traps, on UW–Madison Vegetable Entomology's PVY page, gave a window from about 1,838 to 3,266 degree days; the refitted curve puts the peak later.[^agweather-pvy][^uw-vegento-pvy]
