The common asparagus beetle (_Crioceris asparagi_) is a leaf beetle that feeds on asparagus. It and the spotted asparagus beetle (_Crioceris duodecimpunctata_) are pests of asparagus every year in Wisconsin, but the common asparagus beetle is found more often and is the only one of the two that consistently causes economic damage.[^uw-hort][^umn] Adults scar the spears during harvest, and adults and larvae strip the ferns in summer, which weakens the planting for the following year.[^uw-hort]

## Identification

- **Adults** are slender beetles about ¼ inch long. The common asparagus beetle is bluish black with six cream-colored spots. The spotted asparagus beetle is reddish orange with twelve black spots; don't confuse it with lady beetles, which are rounder, with the head mostly hidden from above.[^umn][^uw-hort]
- **Eggs** of both species are shiny, dark rods about ⅛ inch long. The common asparagus beetle lays them in rows on spears, ferns and flower buds; the spotted species lays them singly on ferns.[^uw-hort][^umn]
- **Larvae** are plump, wrinkled and slug-like, with visible legs. Those of the common asparagus beetle are cream to gray with a dark head and legs; those of the spotted species are orange.[^uw-hort][^umn]

## Life cycle

Both species spend the winter as adults in sheltered places in and near the planting: plant debris, mulch, and the hollow stems of old ferns.[^uw-hort][^umn] Common asparagus beetles become active as the spears come up, in April or early May, earlier than the spotted species, which often doesn't appear until late May.[^a3422-2026][^uw-hort] Adults feed on the spears and lay eggs on them. The eggs hatch in about a week, and the larvae feed for about two weeks, then drop to the soil to pupate. New adults emerge about a week later. Two to three generations develop in a season, the later ones feeding on the ferns.[^uw-hort][^umn] Adults and larvae are most active on warm, sunny afternoons.[^uw-hort][^umn]

Like other insects, asparagus beetles develop faster in warm weather, so their stages can be followed with [degree days](/topics/degree-days), a running total of warmth above a base temperature.

## Damage

- **On spears:** adult feeding causes browning, scarring and bent, hooked tips. Eggs and the larvae's black droppings on spears are harmless to eat but make the spears unmarketable.[^uw-hort][^umn][^uw-vegento]
- **On ferns:** after harvest, adults and larvae feed on the ferns. Heavy feeding can strip the plants, which reduces the next years' yields; the weakened plants are also more open to Fusarium.[^uw-hort][^umn]
- **New plantings** tolerate less injury than established ones.[^uw-hort]

## Monitoring

- Scout from spear emergence in spring through the summer, in the afternoon, when beetles are most active.[^uw-hort][^umn]
- In large plantings, check 20 plants at each of five places in spring, and 10 plants at each of five places in summer.[^uw-hort]
- UW–Madison's thresholds for treatment: adults on 5 to 10 % of plants, eggs on 2 % of spears, larvae on 50 to 75 % of plants, or 10 % of plants defoliated. Spring thresholds protect the spears; summer thresholds protect the ferns, and next year's crop, from defoliation.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Sanitation:** remove or destroy the ferns once they're completely brown in fall, or as early as you can in spring, and other crop residue, to take away the beetles' winter shelter.[^umn][^uw-hort]
- **Prompt harvest:** pick spears promptly and regularly, which leaves fewer spears for egg laying.[^umn]
- **Natural enemies:** a tiny parasitic wasp, _Tetrastichus asparagi_, lays its eggs in those of the common asparagus beetle and can kill up to 70 % of them. Lady beetle larvae and other predators eat eggs and larvae. Insecticides kill these allies too.[^uw-hort][^umn]
- **Row covers** keep beetles off spears in early spring; take them off when harvest is over.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento]
- **In home gardens,** hand-pick adults or knock them into soapy water, and crush the eggs.[^uw-hort][^umn]

**Insecticides:** you don't need to treat a whole planting; spot-treat the areas over threshold.[^uw-hort] During harvest, choose a product with a short preharvest interval, and spray when beetles are active on sunny afternoons. Spears that come up after a spray aren't protected, so keep scouting.[^umn] Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin lists carbamates (IRAC group 1A; carbaryl, methomyl), organophosphates (group 1B; malathion, and dimethoate after the last harvest) and a pyrethroid (group 3A; permethrin). Spinosyns (group 5; spinetoram, and spinosad on ferns only) have preharvest intervals of 60 days, so they suit the ferns rather than the spears.[^a3422-2026] In home gardens, neem (azadirachtin, group UN, mode of action uncertain), insecticidal soap, pyrethrins (group 3A) and spinosad (group 5) are organic options.[^uw-hort][^irac-moa] Rotate classes, and protect bees: asparagus ferns in flower attract them.[^umn]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [asparagus beetle page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/asparagus-beetle/) and the University of Minnesota's [asparagus beetle page](https://extension.umn.edu/yard-and-garden-insects/asparagus-beetles) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see UW–Madison's [asparagus beetle fact sheet](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/asparagus-beetle/).

## AgWeather's models

The [common asparagus beetle model](/models/common-asparagus-beetle) counts degree days from January 1 by UW–Madison's method (base 50 °F, upper limit 86 °F). It follows the overwintered beetles feeding and laying eggs on spears, with 10 % active at about 150 degree days and half at about 240, the span UW–Madison's degree-day table gives for egg laying.[^uw-degree-days] Larvae follow the eggs, and new generations of beetles follow about every 600 degree days, up to three in a season. The egg's week and the time between generations are our estimates from UW–Madison's development times in days, and the larvae's feeding time is a placeholder somewhat longer than UW–Madison's two weeks; none comes from a degree-day study.[^uw-hort][^agweather-common-asparagus-beetle] Both beetles and larvae count in the model's risk: beetles scar spears and their eggs make spears unmarketable during harvest, and larvae feed on spears and ferns.

We worked out what the model means on the calendar from AgWeather's long-term averages (degree days for 1996 to 2025): in an average year the beetles become active around April 28 at Arlington and May 8 at Rhinelander, and half are out by May 10 and May 20, in line with UW–Madison's and Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin's April to early May.[^uw-hort][^a3422-2026] The second generation's beetles peak in late June, and the third in late July to early August. The model hasn't been compared with field counts. Scout the spears from emergence, and the ferns all summer, whatever the model shows.
