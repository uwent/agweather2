Tar spot is a leaf disease of corn caused by the fungus _Phyllachora maydis_. It was first confirmed in the United States in 2015, in northern Indiana and Illinois, and was first found in Wisconsin in 2016.[^webster-2023][^a3646-2025] It has since become established across much of the Midwest. In years when the weather favors it, tar spot can kill leaves early and cause serious yield losses in susceptible hybrids.[^cpn-overview-2026][^a3646-2025]

## Identification

- **Stromata:** the disease is named for small, raised, black spots called stromata, 1/16 to 1/4 inch across, round to diamond-shaped. These fruiting bodies of the fungus are embedded in the leaf, so it feels bumpy. They form on both leaf surfaces, and in severe cases on any green part of the plant, including husks and leaf sheaths.[^cpn-overview-2026][^a3646-2025]
- **The rub test:** stromata can't be rubbed or scraped off. Insect droppings and soil rub off and usually sit on one side of the leaf only.[^cpn-overview-2026]
- **Fisheyes:** tan to brown dead tissue with a darker border sometimes forms around stromata. It isn't always present, so don't wait for it to confirm tar spot. In severe cases these dead areas join up and the leaf dies.[^cpn-overview-2026][^a3646-2025]
- **Look-alikes:** late-season rust pustules turn dark and raised and can be hard to tell from stromata without a laboratory. Physoderma brown spot is flat rather than raised and is concentrated near the base of the leaf; tar spot is spread along the whole blade and is often denser toward the tip.[^cpn-overview-2026]

If you aren't sure, send a sample to UW–Madison's [Plant Disease Diagnostics Clinic](https://pddc.wisc.edu/) or ask your county extension office.[^pddc]

## Life cycle

The fungus spends the winter in infected corn residue, where stromata can survive a season and release spores the next year. Spores are released in rain and high humidity and spread mostly by rain splash and wind over short distances, though longer-distance spread also seems to occur. New stromata appear about 12 to 21 days after infection, so the disease can cycle several times in one summer.[^cpn-overview-2026]

Corn can be infected at any stage, and early infections can show as soon as V5 or V6 (five or six leaf collars), but tar spot is seen most often from silking through the end of grain fill. It is favored by moderate temperatures of about 60 to 74 °F and by alternating wet and dry periods; prolonged high humidity and excessive rain are linked to less disease, not more.[^cpn-overview-2026] A study in eight states, including Wisconsin, found that tar spot increased most after a month or more of mean temperatures between 64 and 73 °F, and less after long spells of nights above 90 % relative humidity.[^webster-2023]

## Damage

Tar spot reduces the leaf area that fills the grain. Moderate epidemics can cut yield by about 10 to 30 %, and severe ones by half. Losses are greatest when disease builds up before or during early grain fill (tasseling, VT, to milk, R3); disease that arrives after the dough or dent stage usually costs little.[^cpn-overview-2026]

Once about 30 % of a leaf's area is affected, the leaf may die early. Badly affected plants dry down abnormally fast, fill kernels poorly, and are more prone to stalk rot and lodging.[^cpn-overview-2026][^smith-2021] In silage, severe tar spot lowers whole-plant moisture and can make packing and fermentation harder. No mycotoxins have been linked to tar spot itself.[^cpn-overview-2026][^smith-2021]

## Monitoring

- Scout fields weekly from tasseling, looking for raised black spots on both sides of the leaves, starting with susceptible hybrids and late-planted fields.[^cpn-overview-2026][^a3646-2025]
- The [Corn ipmPIPE map](https://corn.ipmpipe.org/tar-spot/) shows the counties where tar spot has been confirmed during the season.[^ipmpipe][^cpn-overview-2026]
- In heavily diseased fields, check stalk strength with a push test before harvest, and harvest weak fields first to limit lodging.[^cpn-overview-2026][^smith-2021]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls** reduce the risk but don't prevent tar spot:

- Choose hybrids with partial resistance. No hybrid is fully resistant, but some are much less susceptible than others; compare seed company ratings and local university hybrid trials.[^cpn-overview-2026][^smith-2021][^a3646-2025]
- Rotation and tillage reduce the residue the fungus overwinters in, but they play only a minor role, because spores also blow in. Tar spot has appeared in fields rotated from soybean with no history of the disease.[^cpn-overview-2026][^a3646-2025]
- Plant at the population that gives the best economic yield: in trials, low seeding rates led to more tar spot. Nitrogen rate made no difference in Michigan trials. Under irrigation, avoid frequent light watering.[^cpn-overview-2026]

**Fungicides** can protect yield if applied near the start of the epidemic: even an effective product does poorly once the disease has built up.[^smith-2021][^cpn-efficacy-2026]

- **Timing:** a single application between tasseling and milk (VT to R3) has given the most consistent protection. A fungicide protects the leaves for about 2 to 5 weeks, so a spray made well before tasseling may wear off before grain fill; in high-pressure years, two applications may be needed. An application at dough (R4) can still help where 5 % or more of the leaf area is affected.[^cpn-overview-2026][^smith-2021]
- **Classes:** products for tar spot come from three groups: quinone outside inhibitors (QoIs, FRAC group 11), demethylation inhibitors (DMIs, group 3) and succinate dehydrogenase inhibitors (SDHIs, group 7). Products that combine two or three modes of action control tar spot more consistently than single-group products.[^cpn-efficacy-2026][^smith-2021]
- **Restrictions:** fungicides have harvest intervals, and some differ for silage and grain. Check the label before a late application.[^cpn-efficacy-2026]

For products and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf) and the Corn Disease Working Group's [fungicide efficacy ratings](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/publications/fungicide-efficacy-for-control-of-corn-diseases). UW–Madison's [Tar Spot of Corn Is Here To Stay](https://ipcm.wisc.edu/blog/2021/10/tar-spot-of-corn-is-here-to-stay/) and the Crop Protection Network's [An Overview of Tar Spot](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/publications/an-overview-of-tar-spot) explain the choices in more detail.

## AgWeather's models

The [tar spot model](/models/tar-spot) averages two equations from Webster et al. (2023): one uses the last 30 days' mean temperature and mean daily maximum humidity, the other the same temperature and the last 14 days' humid night hours. We scale the result down when the last three weeks' nights have been cold.[^webster-2023][^agweather-tar-spot]

The equations were fitted to whether tar spot severity had increased between two ratings of the same field, in small plots and commercial fields from 2018 to 2022. So the model gives the probability that recent weather has favored tar spot increasing, not the chance that the fungus is present. Averaged, the two equations classified about 87 % of the study's test observations correctly. In both, a warmer month and longer humid nights lower the probability, in line with the study's finding that long spells above about 73 °F and long periods of high humidity discourage tar spot.[^webster-2023][^agweather-tar-spot]

The risk is shown from V10 (ten leaf collars) to milk, using the [corn stages model](/models/corn-stages), or your own planting date at a location. Our version reproduces the Crop Risk Tool it replaces exactly, but it hasn't been compared with tar spot in Wisconsin fields, and we estimate humid hours from gridded weather, which may change the risk by an amount not yet measured.[^agweather-tar-spot] Use it alongside scouting: it can't tell you whether tar spot is in your field.
