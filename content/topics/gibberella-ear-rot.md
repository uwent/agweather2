Gibberella ear rot is a disease of corn ears caused by the fungus _Fusarium graminearum_, also known by the name of its sexual stage, _Gibberella zeae_. With Fusarium ear rot, it is one of the two most common ear rots in Wisconsin.[^a3646-2025] The fungus produces toxins in the grain, chiefly deoxynivalenol (DON, also called vomitoxin) and zearalenone, which harm livestock.[^woloshuk-2010][^cpn-ger][^cpn-mycotoxin-2025] The same fungus causes Gibberella stalk rot of corn and Fusarium head blight (scab) of wheat.[^webster-2021][^woloshuk-2010]

## Identification

- **On the ear:** a pink to reddish mold starts at the tip of the ear and spreads toward the base. Except in very susceptible hybrids, it usually affects only part of the ear.[^woloshuk-2010]
- **Severe cases:** much of the ear rots, the husks and silks stick tightly to it, and pink mold grows between the husks and the ear or shows on the outside of the husks at the tip.[^woloshuk-2010][^cpn-ger]
- **When to look:** it is easiest to see on intact ears just before harvest, and much harder to recognize once the grain is shelled.[^woloshuk-2010]

## Life cycle

The fungus spends the winter on corn and wheat residue. Rain splash and wind carry its spores to the silks, and it infects the ear through them. Silks are most susceptible two to six days after they emerge, and most infections start during pollination (R1), while the silks are wet. Insects feeding on ears later in the season open another, less common route.[^woloshuk-2010][^cpn-ger][^hartschuh-2026]

Gibberella ear rot is more common when the three weeks after silking are cool and wet, and long rainy spells in the fall that slow drydown make it worse.[^woloshuk-2010] In Wisconsin, ear rots are associated with above-normal rain from July through October, insect feeding on ears, severe leaf disease and hail.[^a3646-2025] The fungus makes its toxins mostly at 50 to 70 °F while kernel moisture is above 20 %, as in many falls and when wet grain waits in bins before drying.[^a3646-2025] Risk is highest for a susceptible hybrid planted no-till into corn residue, or after wheat that had scab.[^hartschuh-2026][^woloshuk-2010]

## Damage

Ear rot lowers the yield, quality and feeding value of grain, and its toxins harm livestock.[^a3646-2025]

- **DON** causes feed refusal, vomiting and poor weight gain, and suppresses the immune system.[^cpn-mycotoxin-2025] Swine are especially sensitive.[^woloshuk-2010]
- **Zearalenone** acts like estrogen and can cause infertility and other breeding problems; as little as 1 to 5 parts per million (ppm) in a ration may affect swine.[^woloshuk-2010]
- **Limits:** the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's advisory levels for DON are 1 ppm in finished wheat products for people; 5 ppm in grain for swine, as no more than 20 % of the diet; 10 ppm in grain for chickens, as no more than half the diet; and 10 ppm in grain for beef, feedlot and dairy cattle older than four months, with the whole ration below 10 ppm for beef cattle and 5 ppm for dairy cattle.[^fda-don] Dairy nutritionists suggest a lower limit for dairy cattle, 0.5 to 1 ppm in the whole diet.[^smith-2018]

How moldy the ears look is a poor guide to the toxin. Fields with little visible ear rot can still produce grain high in DON, and in silage corn DON can build up in the stalks even when the ears look clean.[^smith-2018][^cpn-mycotoxin-2025][^webster-2021]

## Monitoring

- Before harvest, peel back the husks of at least 10 ears in several parts of each field and look for pink mold at the tips. Start with fields that silked during cool, wet weather, and plan to harvest them first.[^woloshuk-2010][^hartschuh-2026]
- Test grain if more than 10 % of ears are moldy, and test all grain you will feed to livestock. Test silage corn for DON even if the ears look clean.[^cpn-mycotoxin-2025][^webster-2021]
- For a sample that represents the load, take several subsamples from the moving stream as grain is loaded or unloaded, or probe a load in several places, and combine them into at least 4.4 pounds. Use a laboratory or a test kit: looking at the grain, even under a black light, can't confirm mycotoxins.[^cpn-sampling-2025][^cpn-mycotoxin-2025]
- If you have crop insurance, contact your agent before you harvest, store or sell moldy corn.[^smith-mitchell-2016]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls** do most of the work:

- Choose hybrids less susceptible to ear rot. Partially resistant hybrids have lower DON at harvest than susceptible ones, even when both are sprayed. Tight husk cover, ears that droop at maturity and full-season hybrids all go with less ear rot.[^hartschuh-2026][^a3646-2025]
- Rotate crops and manage residue: Gibberella ear rot is more common where corn residue is left on the surface through a wet season.[^a3646-2025]
- Control European corn borer and corn earworm where practical; insect feeding opens ears to infection.[^a3646-2025][^cpn-ger]
- Harvest affected fields early, because toxin risk grows as harvest drags on. Dry grain promptly to 15 % moisture for winter storage and 13 % or less for longer storage. Drying stops the fungus but doesn't remove toxins already in the grain, so don't blend moldy grain with clean grain.[^a3646-2025][^cpn-mycotoxin-2025][^smith-mitchell-2016]

**Fungicides** can lower DON somewhat, if timed to protect the silks:

- **Timing:** apply in early to mid R1, when most of the field is silking but before the first silks turn brown. Once silks have dried and browned, a fungicide does little; in Ohio State's research, an application 11 days after full silking left as much DON as no spray. If weather threatens to delay you, spraying a little early is better than late.[^hartschuh-2026]
- **Classes:** products labeled to suppress Gibberella ear rot and DON include demethylation inhibitors (DMIs, FRAC group 3) such as prothioconazole, alone or in premixes.[^hartschuh-2026][^webster-2021][^cpn-efficacy-2026]
- **Results vary:** in Wisconsin silage trials, prothioconazole and a premix of pyraclostrobin (a QoI, group 11) and metconazole (a DMI, group 3) applied at silking reduced leaf disease but lowered DON inconsistently.[^webster-2021][^frac-2026] A fungicide supports, but doesn't replace, resistant hybrids and testing.

For products and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf). Ohio State's [vomitoxin management article](https://agcrops.osu.edu/newsletter/corn-newsletter/2026-23/vomitoxin-management-and-corn-fungicide-decisions-during) and the Crop Protection Network's [Corn Mycotoxin FAQs](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/publications/mycotoxin-faqs) and [sampling and testing guide](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/publications/grain-and-silage-sampling-and-mycotoxin-testing) explain the choices in more detail.

## AgWeather's models

The [Gibberella ear rot DON model](/models/gibberella-ear-rot-don) estimates the probability that grain will contain more than 1 ppm of DON at harvest, from the temperature and rainy days in the two weeks ending a week before silking and the humidity in the two weeks before silking. Its equation comes from Ohio State's cereal pathology program, through the Crop Risk Tool, and our version reproduces that tool exactly.[^agweather-don][^hartschuh-2026] The risk is shown at silking (R1), using the date from the [corn stages model](/models/corn-stages) or your own planting date at a location; if your field silks on a different day, read the risk for that day.[^agweather-don]

The equation hasn't been published, so we can't check where, when or on which hybrids it was fitted. Some of its terms run against the extension sources: more rainy days and more very humid days each lower the probability, though wet weather around silking favors the disease. The model hasn't been compared with DON in Wisconsin grain, and a day or two of error in the silking date shifts its windows.[^agweather-don] Like Ohio State's own forecasts, it says whether conditions favor DON above 1 ppm, not how much to expect.[^hartschuh-2026] Whatever it shows, test grain and silage before you feed or sell them.
