The western bean cutworm (_Striacosta albicosta_) is a moth whose caterpillars feed on the kernels inside corn ears, in both field and sweet corn. As its name says, it also attacks dry beans, but not soybeans. Long a pest of the High Plains and western Corn Belt, it has spread east into the Midwest since 2000 and was first found in Wisconsin in 2005. Damage in the state has been sporadic, mostly low to moderate.[^uw-vegento]

## Identification

- **Adults** are brown moths about ¾ inch long with a wingspan of about 1½ inches. Each front wing has three marks: a white stripe along the front edge, a round spot near the center and a boomerang-shaped spot toward the tip.[^a3646-2025]
- **Eggs** are laid in tight masses of 20 to 200 on the upper surface of corn leaves. They turn from creamy white to tan, then deep purple 12 to 24 hours before they hatch.[^a3646-2025]
- **Caterpillars** are dull orange with a black head when they hatch. Full grown, they are about 1½ inches long and tan, with two broad brown stripes just behind the head. They lack the dark spines, bumps and side stripes of corn earworms.[^a3646-2025]

## Life cycle

There is one generation a year. Full-grown caterpillars spend the winter in chambers in the soil, start developing again once temperatures pass 50 °F and pupate in May. Moths begin to emerge in late June or early July, depending on the season's warmth.[^a3646-2025] In the Upper Midwest the flight usually peaks in the second or third week of July.[^uw-vegento]

Females prefer corn just before tasseling and lay eggs on the topmost leaf and on leaves near the developing ear.[^a3646-2025] The eggs hatch 5 to 7 days later. The young caterpillars first eat their eggshells, then feed on pollen in the whorl or tassel, then move to the green silks and into the ear.[^uw-vegento] Unlike corn earworms, they don't eat each other, so several can feed in one ear; latecomers chew in through the side of the husk.[^uw-vegento][^a3646-2025] By early September the grown caterpillars drop to the ground and dig in for the winter.[^uw-vegento] Heavy rain, cold and strong winds kill many young caterpillars.[^uw-vegento]

## Damage

- **Corn:** caterpillars eat kernels at the tip, middle and base of the ear. Their feeding opens the way for molds and ear rots.[^uw-vegento][^a3646-2025] Heavy infestations have cut yields by up to 40 % in Colorado.[^uw-vegento]
- **Dry beans:** caterpillars feed on leaves and flowers and chew into pods and seeds.[^uw-vegento]
- Damage shows from early August to early September. It's easiest to identify in August, while the caterpillars are still in the ears; once they leave, it's hard to tell from that of corn earworm or corn borer.[^uw-vegento]

## Monitoring

- **Pheromone traps** catch male moths. Set them up at the field edge by July 1, check them at least weekly until catches fall off in August, and replace lures after four weeks. UW Vegetable Entomology describes how to make one from a milk jug.[^uw-vegento] Wisconsin's Department of Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection (DATCP) runs a statewide [trap network](https://datcp.wi.gov/Pages/Programs_Services/TrapNetworks.aspx) and posts the counts and maps.[^datcp-traps]
- **When to scout:** start when degree days point to 25 % of the moths having flown, or when the first moths are caught in traps nearby, and keep going for 7 to 10 days after the peak.[^a3646-2025][^uw-vegento]
- **How to scout:** check 20 plants in a row at five places in the field, looking at the top three or four leaves and the leaves around the ear for egg masses and small caterpillars. Scout tasseling fields first, and fields at different stages separately. Check the non-Bt refuge too.[^a3646-2025][^uw-vegento][^bick-2026]
- **Thresholds:** consider a spray when 5 % of field corn plants, or 4 % of processing sweet corn plants, have egg masses or small caterpillars.[^a3646-2025][^bick-2026]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Natural controls:** lady beetles, damsel bugs and spiders eat the caterpillars, and weather kills many more.[^uw-vegento]
- **Tillage** may kill some overwintering caterpillars, but it isn't proven enough to recommend.[^uw-vegento]
- **Bt corn:** not every Bt hybrid controls western bean cutworm, so check the label.[^uw-vegento][^a3646-2025] Hybrids with the Cry1F protein have lost their effect where the insect has become resistant; Vip3A is the only Bt protein that still gives reliable protection.[^unl-2020]

**Insecticides** work only before caterpillars enter the ear. If eggs have hatched in a field over the threshold, spray after 95 % of the tassels have emerged but before the caterpillars reach the silks. If eggs haven't hatched yet and the corn has tasseled, spray as close to hatch as you can, when the egg masses have turned dark purple.[^a3646-2025] Sprays can go on by air or with high-clearance ground rigs.[^uw-vegento] UW–Madison's field crop guide lists pyrethroids (IRAC group 3A), diamides (group 28, chlorantraniliprole) and insect growth regulators (group 18, methoxyfenozide), the last also premixed with a spinosyn (group 5, spinetoram).[^a3646-2025]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf) for field corn and [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/) for sweet corn, or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [western bean cutworm page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/western-bean-cutworm/) and the University of Nebraska–Lincoln's [scouting and treatment recommendations](https://cropwatch.unl.edu/2020/scouting-and-treatment-recommendations-western-bean-cutworm) explain the choices in more detail.

## AgWeather's models

The [western bean cutworm model](/models/western-bean-cutworm) follows the flight model Hanson and colleagues fitted in 2015 to black light trap catches at four sites in Nebraska, 1981–2009, which NEWA also uses. It always counts degree days from March 1, the start the model was fitted from, as the day's average temperature above 38 °F, with the average capped at 75 °F. A quarter of the moths have flown by about 2,560 degree days, half by about 2,705 and three quarters by about 2,850.[^hanson-2015][^newa-wbc] The model shows the whole flight as a curve, times the egg masses that follow it, and marks a window to scout for egg masses from the first moths (5 % of the flight) to 90 %. On the 1996–2025 normals, half the moths have flown by about July 27 at Arlington, August 2 at Hancock and August 13 at Rhinelander; recent, warmer years ran earlier.[^agweather-wbc]

Against DATCP's Wisconsin pheromone traps, 2021–2026, the model's date for half the flight fell a median 3 days before the end of the week in which half the season's moths were caught, and within a week of it in almost every trap-year.[^datcp-traps][^agweather-wbc] NEWA's version counts degree days a little differently from the published method and puts the flight about 3 days later.[^agweather-wbc] Moths blown in by storms can arrive outside the model's dates, so confirm the flight with your own traps or DATCP's before scouting.
