Potato volunteers are potato plants (_Solanum tuberosum_) that grow from tubers left in a field after harvest. Tubers that survive the winter sprout in the next season's crop, often corn, soybean, a small grain or dry beans, where they compete as weeds and carry potato pests and diseases into fields that aren't managed for potato.[^uw-vegento-dsr][^cohen-2024] Whether they survive depends mostly on how cold the soil gets around them over the winter. This page explains why volunteers matter, how winter weather decides their numbers, and how to manage them.

## Why volunteers matter

- **Late blight.** The late blight pathogen survives between seasons in infected potato tubers: seed, cull piles and volunteers. The strains in North America don't leave long-lived spores in the soil. Volunteers in fields and field edges don't get the protective fungicides a potato crop does, so they can start an outbreak.[^uw-vegpath-2025][^uw-vegento-dsr] Where volunteers may come from a field that had late blight the year before, earlier late blight management may be needed.[^gevens-2026]
- **Viruses.** Volunteers can carry potato virus Y and other viruses that aphids spread into seed potato fields. UW–Madison's guidance for seed growers is to make sure no volunteers emerge in fields that grew potatoes before.[^uw-vegento-pvy][^ozturk-2023]
- **Insects.** Colorado potato beetle adults feed on volunteers in spring and then move into production fields.[^cohen-2024]
- **Weeds that are hard to kill.** A volunteer can resprout from its tuber after its leaves are destroyed, and it makes new daughter tubers that can come up for years.[^hutchinson-2016][^cohen-2024]

## How winter weather drives survival

Tubers die when the soil around them freezes hard enough for long enough. A rule developed at Michigan State University by Willie Kirk and Phil Wharton counts the hours with soil below 27 °F between November 1 and March 31, at 2 and 4 inches deep. More than 120 hours at both depths means survival is unlikely (low risk); more than 120 at 2 inches only, moderate risk; fewer than 120 at both, high risk.[^cohen-2024][^uw-vegpath-2025]

Shallow tubers freeze first. A tuber deeper than about 4 inches is unlikely to get cold enough to die.[^cohen-2024] Snow insulates the soil, so a cold winter with deep snow can still leave tubers alive, while an open winter with little snow lets frost go deep.[^uw-vegpath-2024][^uw-vegpath-2025] Volunteers are a bigger problem after mild winters.[^ozturk-2023] Winters differ a great deal: at UW–Madison's Hancock Agricultural Research Station, the soil never fell below 27 °F in one warm, snowy winter, while in a colder one with little snow it stayed below 27 °F from December through March.[^uw-vegpath-2024][^uw-vegpath-2025]

## Managing volunteers

Prevention is the cheapest control, since no herbicide kills volunteers completely.[^cohen-2024]

**At and after harvest:**

- Set harvesters to leave as few tubers as possible in the field.[^ozturk-2023]
- Leave tubers on or near the surface over winter, where they're most likely to freeze. Till shallowly, or wait until spring, since tubers deep in the soil are insulated from the cold.[^ozturk-2023]
- Spread cull potatoes thinly on the surface so they freeze, and don't cover tubers with heavy layers of manure or other organic material.[^ozturk-2023] In Wisconsin, cull piles must be disposed of by May 20 each year (ATCP 21.15) to keep late blight from starting in them.[^uw-vegpath-2025]

**In the next crop:**

- Scout fields that grew potatoes the year before, and field edges, as volunteers come up.[^uw-vegento-pvy]
- Time herbicides to tuber initiation, when the tips of the underground stems start to swell. Earlier, the mother tuber may resprout; later, daughter tubers may already have formed. Most labels promise suppression, not control, and repeated applications or an herbicide followed by cultivation work better than one pass.[^hutchinson-2016][^ozturk-2023]
- Choose herbicides by the crop the volunteers are in. University of Idaho recommendations include glyphosate in glyphosate-tolerant crops; mesotrione, dicamba, fluroxypyr and atrazine in corn; fluroxypyr, bromoxynil and dicamba in small grains; and imazamox or bentazon in dry beans.[^hutchinson-2016][^cohen-2024] A product must be labeled for the crop or site where the volunteers emerge.[^gevens-2026]
- Cultivate repeatedly, starting when volunteers are 3 to 6 inches tall; at least four cultivations are recommended. In research, cultivating four times, each time the volunteers regrew, nearly stopped new tubers from forming, though cultivation doesn't reach volunteers in the crop row.[^ozturk-2023][^hutchinson-2016]

For more, see UW–Madison Vegetable Pathology's [potato volunteer risk article](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/2025/04/22/potato-volunteer-risk/) and the University of Maine's [Management of Volunteer Potatoes](https://extension.umaine.edu/publications/1090e/). For the herbicides labeled in Wisconsin field crops, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf), which doesn't rate them on volunteer potato, and always follow the product label.

## AgWeather's models

The [potato volunteer survival model](/models/potato-volunteer) applies Michigan State's rule to AgWeather's soil temperatures. It counts the hours below 27 °F at 2, 4 and 8 inches from November 1 through March 31; the 8-inch depth comes from the Wisconsin potato volunteer tool, which this model replaces. Each depth with at least 120 hours lowers the chance that tubers survive by one level, from Very high (none) to Low (all three). The winter's map appears on January 1 and keeps its final count until the next winter.[^agweather-potato-volunteer]

Our soil temperatures come from a weather model, not from soil sensors. Compared with Wisconet's stations over a winter and a half, they count more hours below 27 °F, about 40 % more at 2 inches and twice as many at 8 inches, so our risk may run lower than the stations' would. Wisconet's stations sit in grassy areas outside fields, where snow insulates the soil, and a field the wind has swept bare can be colder, so neither is exactly your field. The 120-hour rule comes from Michigan and hasn't been checked against tuber survival in Wisconsin fields. Scout for volunteers whatever the model shows.[^agweather-potato-volunteer][^uw-vegpath-2025]
