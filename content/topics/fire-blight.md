Fire blight is a disease caused by the bacterium _Erwinia amylovora_. It is the most destructive bacterial disease of the rose family and one of the most damaging diseases of apple and pear. It also infects crabapple, quince, hawthorn, mountain-ash, cotoneaster, firethorn and other relatives. It can kill blossoms, shoots, branches and whole trees.

## Identification

- **Blossom blight:** flowers and young fruit wilt and turn dark brown to black. Dead clusters stay on the tree.
- **Shoot blight:** the tips of young shoots blacken, wilt and bend over into a "shepherd's crook". The leaves look scorched and stay attached.
- **Cankers:** dead areas of bark on branches and trunks, with reddish brown wood beneath. They ooze sticky, amber droplets full of bacteria.
- **Rootstock blight:** sunken purple or orange cankers on the rootstock, which can girdle and kill the tree.
- **Fruit:** sticky, brown, sunken or shriveled areas.

## Life cycle

The bacteria survive the winter at the edges of cankers. As spring warms, they multiply and the cankers ooze. Rain splash and insects carry the ooze, and bees and other pollinators bring the bacteria to open flowers. The bacteria multiply on the blossoms and enter through the nectaries, the flower's nectar glands; rain or heavy dew washes them in. They grow over a wide range of temperatures, fastest at about 70 to 77 °F.

So the riskiest time is bloom, when warm days come with rain or heavy dew. Early cultivars bloom first and are at risk first: in southern Wisconsin in late April 2026, warm, wet weather during early bloom made fire blight a concern for those cultivars. From the blossoms, the bacteria spread quickly through the tree's vascular (sap-conducting) tissue. After bloom, they also enter young shoots through wounds. Hail, wind storms and piercing insects make these wounds, and blight that follows such injury is called trauma blight. Symptoms usually show one to three weeks after infection.

## Damage

Blighted blossoms and fruitlets are lost from the current crop. Shoot blight and cankers kill branches, and trunk or rootstock cankers kill trees. Susceptible cultivars such as Gala and Fuji, and susceptible rootstocks such as M.9 and M.26, suffer most. Fast-growing shoots are especially susceptible. There is no cure once a tree is infected.

## Monitoring

- During bloom, follow a fire blight risk forecast for your area. Models such as Cougarblight and MaryBlyt help time blossom sprays and scouting.
- Record the date your first blossoms open.
- Look for cankers while pruning in winter, and for blighted blossoms and shoots in the weeks after bloom.
- After hail or a severe storm in spring or early summer, check susceptible blocks for trauma blight.

## Management

**Nonchemical controls** are the foundation:

- Plant resistant cultivars and rootstocks where you can; the Geneva series of rootstocks resists fire blight better than M.9 and M.26. Plant in well-drained soil.
- Avoid excess nitrogen and heavy winter pruning, which push the fast-growing shoots that blight most easily.
- Cut out cankers while pruning in winter, and remove trees with trunk or rootstock cankers.
- In summer, prune out blighted shoots promptly on dry days, at least 12 inches below the visible symptoms. Disinfect tools between cuts with 70 % alcohol or diluted bleach, and burn or bury the prunings.

**Sprays** protect but don't cure:

- **Copper** at late dormant or early bloom reduces the bacteria on the tree's surfaces. It can russet fruit.
- **Antibiotics** protect open blossoms. Streptomycin works best applied within about a day before an infection; reapply after heavy rain, when new flowers open, or after a few days of high risk. Kasugamycin is an alternative where streptomycin resistance is a concern, and oxytetracycline is generally less effective. Rotate among them to slow resistance, and use them against shoot blight only after a trauma event such as hail.
- **Prohexadione-calcium**, a plant growth regulator, slows shoot growth and reduces shoot blight.
- **Organic programs** use biological products containing the yeast-like fungus _Aureobasidium pullulans_ or _Bacillus_ bacteria, sprayed repeatedly during bloom because they wash off, along with copper early in the season.

For products, timing and current registrations, see the [Midwest Fruit Pest Management Guide](https://edustore.purdue.edu/id-465-w.html). UW–Madison's [Wisconsin apple disease update](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/wisconsin-apple-disease-update-high-risk-conditions-in-wisconsin/) and Cornell's [fire blight fact sheet](https://cals.cornell.edu/integrated-pest-management/outreach-education/fact-sheets/fire-blight-fact-sheet) explain the choices in more detail; for home trees, see UW–Madison's [fire blight fact sheet](https://pddc.wisc.edu/2015/07/23/fire-blight/).

## AgWeather's models

The [fire blight model](/models/fire-blight) runs during bloom. It gives Cougarblight's risk, from the warmth of the last four days and the fire blight history near your orchard, and MaryBlyt's index of how much warmth the open blossoms have had for bacteria to multiply. It also shows the days blossoms were likely wet, and when to expect symptoms after an infection. Bloom is estimated from degree days, or set from your own first bloom date at a location. The model doesn't cover trauma blight, cankers or shoot blight.
