Alfalfa (_Medicago sativa_) is Wisconsin's main forage crop. In recent years Wisconsin farmers have harvested between about 1.3 and 1.8 million acres of alfalfa for hay and haylage, about three quarters of the state's forage acres, and Grant, Vernon, Green and Iowa counties lead the state in alfalfa hay.[^nass-wi-stats] Alfalfa is cut several times a season, and the timing of each cut trades yield against quality and the health of the stand. This page describes the crop's stages and how they set that timing.

## Alfalfa in Wisconsin

The first cutting usually starts around May 20 and the last ends around October 10.[^nass-wi-stats] The first cutting matters most: it gives the largest share of the season's yield (about 43 % in a three-cut system and 31 % in a five-cut system), and its date sets how many more cuttings follow, how far apart they are and how late the last one comes. Its quality also falls faster than that of later cuttings. Delaying the first cutting by 5 days adds about a quarter ton of dry matter per acre but lowers relative feed value (RFV) by about 20 points.[^uw-first-cut]

The calendar is a poor guide to first-cut quality. Near May 20 in Fond du Lac County, RFV has ranged from 144 in one year to 278 in another. UW–Madison suggests judging fields by scissors-cut samples sent for analysis, by PEAQ (below), or by [growing degree days](/topics/degree-days) above 41 °F, and using one to check another.[^uw-first-cut]

## Growth stages

Alfalfa is staged by the most advanced feature on each stem, on a scale of 0 to 9:[^noland-2023]

- **Vegetative (0 to 2):** no buds, flowers or seed pods; stems under 6 inches (stage 0), 6 to 12 inches (1) or longer (2).
- **Bud (3 and 4):** buds on one or two nodes (early bud), then on three or more (late bud).
- **Flower (5 and 6):** one node with an open flower (early flower), then two or more.
- **Seed pod (7 to 9):** green pods on up to three nodes, then on four or more, then mostly brown, ripe pods.

Averaging the stages of many stems gives a field's mean stage, an indicator of forage quality. Quality is highest while the plants are leafy and drops quickly once they turn from vegetative growth to buds and flowers.[^noland-2023] UW–Madison's PEAQ method (Predictive Equations for Alfalfa Quality) estimates the standing crop's RFV from the height of the tallest stem and the stage of the most mature one, measured in four or five spots across a field.[^uw-first-cut] Wilting, harvest and storage then lower RFV by about another 10 to 25 points.[^uw-peaq]

**Cutting schedules** follow from the goal. Cutting at bud to late bud gives the best quality, usually every 32 to 35 days until late August or early September; cutting near full bloom, every 40 to 45 days, gives more tonnage and keeps stands longer.[^bauder-2022] Fields cut every 30 days or less often don't fully restore the energy reserves in their roots between cuttings and are at more risk of winterkill.[^jarek-fall]

**The fall rest period:** after each cutting, alfalfa regrows from reserves stored in its crown and roots, and starts to rebuild them once the new growth is 6 to 8 inches tall.[^newell-fall] To go into winter with full reserves, the last cutting should leave about 900 degree days (base 41 °F), roughly 5 to 6 weeks, before a killing freeze, which is several hours at 24 °F or below. A cutting so late that fewer than 360 degree days remain leaves too little time to regrow and is also safe.[^newell-fall][^gruss-michel-2024] Some UW–Madison material gives 500 and 200 degree days for this rule; those figures come from research in degrees Celsius, and 900 and 360 are the same values in Fahrenheit.[^newell-fall][^jarek-fall] The riskiest time for a fall cutting is mid-September to mid-October in southern Wisconsin and the last half of September in the north. Leave at least 6 inches of stubble to catch snow. Stands 18 to 36 months old, with good potassium levels and good drainage, tolerate a late cutting best.[^jarek-fall]

## What the stages mean for pests and diseases

- **First crop, from about 300 degree days (base 48 °F):** [alfalfa weevil](/topics/alfalfa-weevil) larvae feed on the stem tips. Treat when 40 % of stems show feeding and harvest is more than a week away; otherwise cut early.[^bick-2025]
- **Stubble after the first cutting:** surviving weevil larvae and new adults can stop regrowth. Check the stubble 4 to 5 days after cutting.[^bick-2025][^a3646-2025]
- **Second and third crops, and new seedings:** potato leafhoppers arrive from the south after the first cutting, which usually escapes them. They stunt plants and yellow the leaves in a V from the tip. Sweep weekly; the treatment threshold rises with plant height, from 0.2 leafhoppers per sweep at 3 inches to 2 per sweep at 12 to 14 inches.[^a3646-2025]
- **Second and third crops:** plant bugs, including the [tarnished plant bug](/topics/tarnished-plant-bug), can stunt alfalfa. Treat at three per sweep on plants under 3 inches and five per sweep on taller plants; within 7 to 10 days of harvest, cut early instead.[^a3646-2025]
- **After first flower:** many leaf diseases increase sharply, so cutting on time often avoids the need for a fungicide.[^a3646-2025]

## Monitoring

- Record each field's cutting dates. Degree days, regrowth and the fall rest period all count from the last cutting.[^agweather-alfalfa-growth][^newell-fall]
- Before the first cutting, estimate quality with PEAQ or scissors-cut samples, and check it against degree days.[^uw-first-cut]
- Scout for alfalfa weevil in spring and for potato leafhoppers weekly through the summer.[^bick-2025][^a3646-2025]

## Management

Many problems are settled by the cutting schedule: cutting on time controls the first generation of several insects and limits leaf diseases.[^a3646-2025] Variety choice matters too, since varieties differ in winter hardiness and in their resistance to root and seedling diseases such as Phytophthora and Aphanomyces root rots.[^a3646-2025][^jarek-fall] For products, see UW–Madison's [Pest Management in Wisconsin Field Crops (A3646)](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/files/2025/01/A3646_2025_web.pdf); for fall cutting, see UW–Madison's [Managing fall alfalfa cutting for persistence](https://cropsandsoils.extension.wisc.edu/articles/managing-fall-alfalfa-cutting-for-persistence/) and Iowa State's [late fall cutting article](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/post/should-i-take-late-fall-alfalfa-cutting-it-too-late-or-warm).

## AgWeather's models

- The [alfalfa growth model](/models/alfalfa-growth) counts growing degree days (single sine, 41 to 86 °F) from January 1 in spring, or from your last cutting, and marks the crop ready to cut from 900 to 1,100 degree days and past the window after that. It reproduces the formula of UW–Madison's alfalfa cutting tool, which it replaces. The cutting window is a rule of thumb that hasn't been compared with forage quality measured in Wisconsin fields, and counting from January 1 includes warm winter days before the crop greens up. The fall rest period isn't modelled yet.[^agweather-alfalfa-growth]
- The [alfalfa weevil model](/models/alfalfa-weevil) follows the weevil's stages from January 1; its page explains its timing and limits.
