The codling moth (_Cydia pomonella_) is a small moth whose larvae tunnel into apples and pears, and occasionally quince and walnuts. It is well established in Wisconsin. Left uncontrolled, it can cause severe losses, and it has become resistant to a number of common insecticides.

## Identification

- **Adults** are gray moths about 3/8 to ½ inch long. The wings are darker near the body, with a patch of coppery scales at the tips.
- **Eggs** are flat, oval discs, laid one at a time on or near the fruit.
- **Larvae** are cream-colored at hatch, turning pinkish with a brown head, and grow to about ⅝ inch.

## Life cycle

Codling moths spend the winter as full-grown larvae in cocoons under loose bark or in leaf litter. In Wisconsin they pupate in early May, around pink, and the first moths fly from mid-May, around full bloom. In the state's trap network, checked weekly, the first moth is usually caught about two weeks after full bloom: in late May in the south and later in the north. Moths fly for about eight weeks; each female lays up to 100 eggs, which hatch in about a week. Young larvae bore into the fruit and feed for three to four weeks, then leave it to spin cocoons.

Some of these larvae go on to a second generation, whose moths fly from about mid-July to late August; the rest wait for spring. A third flight can begin in mid-August. Their young don't survive the winter but can still damage fruit late in the season.

Codling moths develop faster in warm weather, so [degree days](/topics/degree-days), a running total of warmth above a base temperature, predict their stages better than calendar dates. For codling moth the base is 50 °F, and the count starts at the biofix, set from the first moths caught in your traps.

## Damage

- **Deep entries:** a larva tunnels to the core and eats the flesh and seeds. The hole, most often at the calyx (blossom) end, is plugged with brown, sawdust-like droppings called frass. These apples are unmarketable.
- **Stings:** shallow, pinprick marks where a young larva bit in and died. They blemish the fruit and lower its grade.

Larvae are exposed only briefly between hatching and boring in, so control depends on timing. Unsprayed backyard apple trees can keep a large population going, a source of moths for commercial orchards as far as three miles away.

## Monitoring

- By pink, hang sticky traps baited with a codling moth sex pheromone lure inside the canopy: about one for every 5 acres, and at least two per block.
- Check traps daily from bloom until the first catch, then weekly. Traps also catch look-alikes such as the maple twig borer.
- Set your biofix from the catches. Guides differ: UW–Madison's uses the first sustained catch (more than one moth on two nights in a row), Penn State's the first moth caught.
- Without mating disruption, Penn State suggests treating when catches pass five moths per trap per week.
- Wisconsin's Department of Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection (DATCP) posts weekly [trap counts](https://datcp.wi.gov/Pages/Programs_Services/AppleOrchardPests.aspx) from cooperating orchards.

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Mating disruption** fills the orchard with the female's sex pheromone so males can't find mates. It works best in regular blocks over 10 acres where numbers are low. Put dispensers up before the first flight. Where numbers are high, add insecticides for a year or two; more than 10 moths in the first flight, in traps with stronger lures, means disruption alone isn't enough.
- **Sanitation:** destroy fallen fruit. Cardboard bands around trunks catch larvae looking for a place to pupate; remove and destroy them.
- **Codling moth granulosis virus** kills larvae that eat it and is approved for organic use. Larvae die slowly, so some stings still occur; it is reapplied every 10 to 14 days.
- **On backyard trees**, bagging fruit soon after petal fall or coating it with kaolin clay helps keep larvae out.

**Insecticides** are timed to egg hatch. For the first generation, larvicides go on about 250 to 300 degree days after the biofix, and egg-killing products such as methoxyfenozide at about 150; for the second, larvicides go on at about 1,250. Two or three sprays may be needed for each generation. Effective classes include diamides, spinosyns, insect growth regulators, neonicotinoids, pyrethroids and organophosphates, with spinosad and kaolin clay for organic production. Broad-spectrum products such as pyrethroids also kill natural enemies of other pests. Rotate classes to slow resistance, and don't spray insecticides during bloom or while bees are foraging.

For products and current registrations, see the [Midwest Fruit Pest Management Guide](https://edustore.purdue.edu/id-465-w.html). See also UW–Madison's [codling moth publication](https://fruit.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/36/2022/01/A4172-Codling-moth.pdf), Penn State's [codling moth page](https://extension.psu.edu/tree-fruit-insect-pest-codling-moth) and, for home trees, the University of Minnesota's [codling moth page](https://extension.umn.edu/yard-and-garden-insects/codling-moths).

## AgWeather's models

The [codling moth model](/models/codling-moth) follows flight and egg hatch through two generations from degree days. It counts from your first trap catch, or from one estimated for your latitude and adjusted to Wisconsin's trap records. It marks the days to protect fruit, from the start of each generation's egg hatch to 95 % hatch, and from early August estimates how large the second generation will be. The model asks for the date of the first moth, not of the first sustained catch.
