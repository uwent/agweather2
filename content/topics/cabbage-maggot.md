The cabbage maggot (_Delia radicum_) is a small fly whose larvae feed on the roots and lower stems of cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, kohlrabi, radish, turnip, rutabaga and cruciferous weeds such as wild mustard. Early transplants and spring root crops suffer most.[^uw-hort] It came from Europe in the early 1800s and does more damage in the northern United States and Canada than farther south.[^umn-vegedge]

## Identification

- **Adults** are ash-gray, bristly flies about half the length of a house fly, with black stripes on the thorax.[^uw-hort]
- **Eggs** are small, white and bullet-shaped, laid in the soil at the base of the stem.[^umass]
- **Larvae** are white, legless maggots that taper toward the head, about ⅓ inch long when grown.[^uw-hort]

The seedcorn maggot looks almost the same at both stages; the two can generally be told apart only under a microscope. Seedcorn maggots prefer the seeds and seedlings of beans, corn and cucurbits, though they also attack cole crop seed at times.[^umn-vegedge]

## Life cycle

Cabbage maggots spend the winter as pupae in the top few inches of soil. In Wisconsin the first flies come out in mid-May, while yellow rocket and forsythia are flowering.[^uw-hort] Eggs hatch in two to seven days, and the maggots feed on roots for three to four weeks before pupating in the soil.[^uw-vegento]

A second generation of flies emerges in late June, and a third can follow in a long, warm season.[^uw-hort] Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin counts three generations in the state and calls the first, from overwintering pupae, the largest and most damaging.[^a3422-2026] Cabbage maggots favor cool, wet conditions.[^uw-hort] Injury from the second generation is usually light, because the maggots prefer cool, moist soil and young, tender plants, but a third generation can damage late plantings.[^umn-vegedge]

Flights can be predicted with [degree days](/topics/degree-days), a running total of warmth above a base temperature. UW–Madison's guides use a base of 43 °F, counted from when the ground thaws: the first flies appear at about 300 degree days, the second generation at about 1,476 and the third at about 2,652.[^uw-hort][^uw-vegento] These numbers rest on Wisconsin trap catches: over five years, spring flies emerged at 290 to 365 degree days, and each later generation took about 1,176 more.[^eckenrode-1972]

## Damage

Maggots tunnel on and inside roots. Seedlings may lose the growing point of the root and be stunted or killed, while transplants turn off-color and wilt in warm weather, often dying slowly.[^uw-hort][^a3422-2026] Radish, turnip and rutabaga roots are scarred by tunnels and become unmarketable.[^umass] The wounds also let in disease organisms, such as the bacteria that cause soft rot and the fungus that causes blackleg.[^uw-vegento]

## Monitoring

- Yellow bowls of soapy water set every 100 feet along field edges catch the flies; count them every four to six days to see whether numbers are rising or falling. Identify the flies carefully, since several look alike.[^uw-hort][^umn-vegedge]
- Check transplants for eggs by stirring the soil at the base of the stem. UMass treats an average of one egg per plant as a sign that significant damage is likely.[^umass]
- Once you see damage, it is too late to control that generation, so economic thresholds aren't useful; management is preventive.[^umn-vegedge]

## Management

**Nonchemical controls:**

- **Planting date:** plant after the spring flight's peak where you can. Plantings made after mid-June usually suffer less damage than earlier ones.[^uw-hort][^a3422-2026]
- **Organic matter:** flies are drawn to freshly tilled soil with decaying plant material or manure. Work in cover crops and green manures at least two to three weeks before planting, and don't plant cole crops where fresh manure has been spread.[^uw-hort][^umn-root-maggots]
- **Row covers** keep flies from laying eggs if they are in place before the flight. Don't use them where brassicas grew last year: flies emerging from pupae in the soil would be trapped underneath.[^umn-root-maggots][^umass]
- **Sanitation and rotation:** work crop residue into the soil right after harvest, rotate away from last year's brassica fields and cover crops, and control brassica weeds that can carry later generations.[^uw-hort][^mwvg-2026]
- **Natural enemies:** ground beetles and rove beetles eat eggs, larvae and pupae, and soil fungi and parasitic wasps also kill maggots.[^umn-vegedge][^umass]

**Insecticides** work only as prevention, applied to the soil before eggs hatch; nothing controls maggots already in the roots.[^uw-hort][^umn-root-maggots] Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin lists soil treatments at or just before planting (in the furrow, in a band, in transplant water or worked into the soil) with a diamide (IRAC group 28; cyantraniliprole), a pyrethroid (group 3A; bifenthrin) or an organophosphate (group 1B; diazinon). Treatments last only four to five days, so they must go on while flies are laying eggs.[^a3422-2026][^irac-moa] Cabbage maggots have shown resistance to diazinon, and spinetoram (a spinosyn, group 5) is labeled for suppression only.[^mwvg-2026] Applying insecticide in a band at the base of the plants, rather than broadcast, spares the ground beetles that prey on maggots.[^uw-hort][^umass]

For products, rates and current registrations, see UW–Madison's [Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin (A3422)](https://vegpath.plantpath.wisc.edu/resources/a3422/), or in neighboring states the [Midwest Vegetable Production Guide](https://mwveguide.org/guide). UW Vegetable Entomology's [cabbage maggot page](https://vegento.russell.wisc.edu/pests/cabbage-maggot/) and the University of Minnesota's [VegEdge cabbage maggot page](https://vegedge.umn.edu/cabbage-maggot) explain the choices in more detail; for home gardens, see UW–Madison's [cabbage maggot fact sheet](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/cabbage-maggot/).

## AgWeather's models

The [cabbage maggot model](/models/cabbage-maggot) is NEWA's cabbage maggot model, counted from January 1 as the day's average temperature above 40 °F.[^newa] Its spring flight follows two years of trap catches in upstate New York: the first flies at about 290 degree days, half at 450 and 95 % by about 700.[^jyoti-2003] Larval feeding is timed from the flight, and later flights follow every 850 degree days, up to the three found in Wisconsin (New York has four). The model marks a window for protecting seedlings and transplants from each flight's first flies to 95 % emergence.[^agweather-cabbage-maggot]

On the same weather at Wisconsin's NEWA stations, the model's flights fall within a day or two of NEWA's. Wisconsin's own trap study agrees on the spring flight, but its generations took about half again as long, which would put the first summer flight about two weeks later, so Wisconsin's summer flights may come later than the model shows.[^eckenrode-1972][^agweather-cabbage-maggot] In an average year at Arlington (degree days for 1996 to 2025), the model's first flies come about May 1 and the second flight peaks about June 18, near the late-June flight UW–Madison describes.[^uw-hort] The model counts the summer flights at half the spring flight's weight in its risk, since the spring flight does the most damage; use its first window to plan spring plantings and row covers, and traps for later flights.
