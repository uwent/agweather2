## What it predicts

Japanese beetle adults skeletonize the leaves of many fruit, vegetable and ornamental plants and feed on flowers, fruit and corn silks. They have one generation a year: grubs spend the winter in the soil under turf, and adults emerge in early summer and feed for six to eight weeks. The model follows the adults through the season:

- **Adult beetles**: how many beetles are active, compared with the peak of the season.
- **Scout for beetles feeding**: the days from the first beetles (about 2 % of the season's beetles) to the end of main activity (94 %).

Risk is the adults' activity, since the adults do the damage. A risk of 1 means as active as at the season's peak.

## How it works

The model is Oregon State University's Japanese beetle model on USPest.org (version 2, 2024). Degree days are counted from January 1 by the single-sine method, base 50 °F, with an upper limit of 92 °F. In Wisconsin few days pass 92 °F, so the limit moves the dates by a day at most.

- **The season's beetles.** Oregon State averaged adult trap catches at four Minnesota sites over seven seasons: 10 % of the beetles were caught by about 1,100 degree days, half by 1,540, 90 % by 1,985 and 94 % by 2,110, the end of main activity. The model's curve passes through those points.
- **The first and last beetles.** The model's first beetles come at about 920 degree days, when Oregon State suggests setting out traps. Oregon State's last beetles are trapped at about 2,700; by then nearly all the model's beetles are done.

In an average year (AgWeather's degree days for 1996 to 2025), the first beetles come about June 29 at Arlington, July 4 at Hancock and July 13 at Rhinelander. Half the season's beetles have been active by about July 29 at Arlington and mid-August at Rhinelander, and main activity ends about August 25 at Arlington and early September at Hancock. In the north, fall freezes end the season first.

## How to read it

- **Look for beetles from late June,** whatever the model shows, and start control as soon as they appear: a few beetles feeding draw in many more.
- Keep checking until numbers fall. Most feeding is in July and August.
- Don't use pheromone traps for control. They show when beetles are flying, but they draw in more beetles than they catch, and damage near traps can increase.
- Well-established plants tolerate a good deal of leaf feeding; young or weak plants, blossoms and ripe fruit suffer most.

## Validation and caveats

- Draft (model version 0.2, beta). Developed from adult trap catches in Minnesota.
- The model's curve follows Oregon State's numbers within about a percentage point. Oregon State checked its model against daily trap catches in Massachusetts that it wasn't built from, and found its dates within about 4 days.
- **On Wisconsin records.** Against 2,818 iNaturalist photographs of adults in Wisconsin from 2016 to 2026, the first 10 % and half of the records came within about 2 days of the model's. The last records ran later than the model's curve, as sightings of beetles still feeding would. The model wasn't fitted to these records.
- **UW–Madison's guides** put the first adults in late June or early July at about 1,000 degree days, as the model does; UW Vegetable Entomology's page says mid-June, a week or two earlier than the model in southern Wisconsin.
- The model shows where the season stands, not how many beetles there are. Numbers vary with things the model doesn't follow: a dry summer, for one, can mean fewer beetles the next year.
- Weather data come from a grid of cells about 1.5 miles (2.5 km) across, not from a station in your field.
