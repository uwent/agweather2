## What it predicts

The mint flea beetle has one generation a year. It spends the winter as eggs in the soil. The larvae feed on mint roots and tunnel into rhizomes in spring, which stunts the mint and turns it purple. The adults emerge in summer, chew small round holes in the leaves, and after two to three weeks of feeding begin laying the eggs that carry the beetle through the winter. No insecticide is registered for the larvae in the soil, so control aims at the adults after most have emerged but before egg laying starts. The model follows:

- **Larval feeding**: how many larvae are feeding in the roots and rhizomes.
- **Adult feeding**: how many adults are feeding on the leaves.
- **Sweep mint for adults**: the days from the first adults emerging until 95 % have emerged.

Risk adds the larvae's and the adults' feeding. A risk of 1 means as many feeding as at either stage's peak.

## How it works

The model is Oregon State University's mint flea beetle model on USPest.org (Berry and others). Degree days are counted in air from January 1 by the single-sine method, base 41 °F, with no upper limit. Oregon State's model has an upper limit of 103 °F, which Wisconsin's weather didn't reach in the years we checked.

- **Larvae.** Eggs begin to hatch at about 405 degree days, and the first larvae leave the rhizomes to pupate at about 900. Oregon State's model gives only when each stage begins; the model spreads hatching over about 550 degree days, from the Pacific Northwest handbook's hatching from early April through May.
- **Adults.** Adults begin to emerge at about 1,475 degree days, Oregon State's last event. The model spreads emergence over about 900 degree days, from the handbook's emergence in late June and July, and keeps each adult feeding for about 600 degree days, three to four weeks in July.

In an average year (AgWeather's degree days for 1996 to 2025), the first larvae leave the rhizomes in early June, and the first adults emerge about June 25 at Arlington, June 29 at Hancock and July 8 at Rhinelander. Half have emerged by about July 3 at Arlington and July 17 at Rhinelander, and the sweeping window closes about July 13 in the south and July 28 in the north.

## How to read it

- Use the window as a reminder to start sweeping for adults, and keep sweeping after it closes if Wisconsin's later emergence (below) holds in your field. Sweep in the early morning, below about 60 °F, when the beetles stay on the leaves, and check the whole field, starting at the margins, where infestations usually begin.
- Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin suggests treating the adults before they lay eggs if larval damage (stunting, purpling in June) was visible; or, without larval damage, if standing mint has more than 25 adults per 100 sweeps; or if standing mint has fewer but stubble after harvest has more than 25 per 100 sweeps.
- Time sprays from your sweep counts: after most adults have emerged, and before females start laying eggs two to three weeks later.

## Validation and caveats

- Draft (model version 0.2, beta). Developed for mint in the Pacific Northwest, where Oregon State lists it as partly validated; the research behind it isn't published online.
- The model's stages begin at Oregon State's numbers. How long hatching and emergence last is our judgment from the Pacific Northwest handbook's calendar, since Oregon State gives only when each begins.
- **Wisconsin's guide disagrees with itself and with the model.** Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin puts emergence in late July, at about 1,100 degree days above 40 °F counted from soil temperatures 6 inches deep. On AgWeather's soil temperatures for 2016 to 2025, that soil total was reached in early June to early July, a week or so before the model's first adults, not in late July. The model's first adults come in late June in southern Wisconsin and early July in the north, two to four weeks before the guide's late July. No field counts in Wisconsin are known to us that would settle it, so sweep from late June and keep sweeping into August.
- The model counts degree days in air, while the larvae and pupae develop in the soil.
- The model shows where the season stands, not how many beetles there are.
- Weather data come from a grid of cells about 1.5 miles (2.5 km) across, not from a station in your field.
