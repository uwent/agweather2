## What it predicts

Cabbage maggot flies lay eggs in the soil at the base of cabbage, broccoli, radish and other cole crops, and the maggots that hatch feed on the roots. Damage can't be stopped once the maggots are in the roots, so protection has to be in place while flies are laying eggs. The model follows each generation through the season:

- **Adult flight**: how many flies are emerging, compared with the spring flight's peak.
- **Larval feeding**: how many maggots are feeding on roots, timed from the flies that laid their eggs.
- **Protect seedlings and transplants during fly flight**: the days from the first flies (about 5 % of a flight) until 95 % have emerged, for each flight.

Risk adds the two stages together. Flight counts because egg laying follows it; feeding counts because it's the damage. The summer flights and their maggots count half as much as the spring generation, which does the most damage. A risk of 1 means as active as the spring flight at its peak.

## How it works

The model starts from NEWA's cabbage maggot model, from Cornell University, and takes a longer time between flights from the trap studies. Degree days are counted from January 1 as the day's average temperature above 40 °F, with no upper limit.

- **The spring flight.** Flies from the overwintering pupae begin emerging at about 290 degree days; a quarter have emerged by 365, half by 450, and 95 % by about 700. These are NEWA's numbers, from two years of trap catches in upstate New York (Jyoti, Shelton and Barnard 2003).
- **Larvae.** Females begin laying eggs a few days after they emerge, and the eggs hatch a few days later: about 245 degree days in all. The maggots then feed for about 580 degree days, three weeks or so in summer (laboratory times reported by Eckenrode and Chapman 1971). Half of the spring flight's maggots are feeding by about 700 degree days.
- **Later flights.** Each generation takes about 1,275 degree days from flight to flight and spreads a little more than the one before. That's the average of two trap studies: the New York study behind NEWA's model, whose flights peaked about 1,210 degree days apart on average, and Eckenrode and Chapman's in Wisconsin, about 1,335 when counted the way this model counts. NEWA's own later flights come every 850 or so, but that number adds up how long each flight lasted rather than the time between flights. The model stops at three flights, the number Eckenrode and Chapman trapped in Wisconsin (New York has four). Half of the second and third flights have emerged by about 1,730 and 3,010 degree days.
- **The end of the season.** After early September, each day at or below 28 °F reduces activity, and four such days within two weeks end the season.

In an average year (AgWeather's degree days for 1996 to 2025), the spring flight's first flies come about May 1 at Arlington, May 5 at Hancock and May 13 at Rhinelander, and its peak about 11 days later. The later flights peak about July 3 and August 13 at Arlington, five days to a week later at Hancock, and about two to three weeks later at Rhinelander. The second flight's first flies come in late June in southern Wisconsin, as UW–Madison's guides describe.

## How to read it

- **Protect spring plantings through the first window.** Have a soil insecticide, treated seed or transplant drench, or row covers in place before the first flies emerge, and keep plantings protected until the window closes. Where you can, plant after the spring flight's peak.
- **Row covers** keep flies off only if they go on before the flight. Don't use them where brassicas grew last year, since flies emerging from the soil would be trapped underneath.
- **Soil treatments last only four to five days,** so time them to the window rather than to the calendar.
- **The spring flight does the most damage.** NEWA and UW–Madison find early plantings most at risk from it, and Commercial Vegetable Production in Wisconsin calls the spring generation the largest and most damaging; later flights can damage late plantings and root crops. The model counts the summer flights at half the spring flight's weight in the risk, a judgment, since the sources say which flight matters most but not by how much. Judge the summer windows with yellow-bowl traps along field edges.

## Validation and caveats

- Draft (model version 0.4, beta). Developed from trap catches in upstate New York in 1999 and 2000, with the time between flights also from Wisconsin trap catches in 1965 to 1969.
- On the same weather at 34 Wisconsin NEWA stations in 2024 and 2025, the model's first flies and spring flight fall on NEWA's days. Its summer flights peak about two and four weeks after NEWA's, because of the longer time between flights.
- **The time between flights is an average of two studies.** In Eckenrode and Chapman's trap catches at Kenosha and Arlington (1965 to 1969), spring flies first emerged on about the same days as the model's first flies, or up to about 9 days later, and a generation took about as long as the model's: the model's first summer flight peaks about 2 days before theirs would. In the New York study's own years, the model's summer peaks came from a day before to 12 days after the trapped peaks, and NEWA's from 7 to 26 days before them. Generations varied from season to season by a few hundred degree days in both studies, so watch traps to confirm the summer flights.
- **A fourth flight.** The model stops at three flights, as found in Wisconsin. In most recent seasons, southern and central Wisconsin had enough warmth for a fourth flight to begin by mid- to late September, which the model doesn't show; late plantings of cole crops and radishes may see flies then.
- The summer flights' half weight in the risk is our judgment. In New York's traps the first summer flight was about as large as the spring flight and later ones a fifth to two-fifths of it or less; in Wisconsin's radish plantings, summer damage was a few percent to about a quarter of spring's.
- The timing of egg laying and larvae comes from laboratory development times, not from field observations.
- Weather data come from a grid of cells about 1.5 miles (2.5 km) across, not from a station in your field.
