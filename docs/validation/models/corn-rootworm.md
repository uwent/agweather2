# Corn rootworm (`corn-rootworm`): validation

Model version 0.2, written 2026-11-21 (D98; part H, batch H4), its sources read in full 2026-11-23. The model: `packages/agmodels/src/agmodels/insects/corn_rootworm.py`, run by the insect engine (`agmodels.phenology`, D36); its page: `corn_rootworm.md` beside it. Version 0.1 was VDIFN's window (329, 472, 636 degree days, single sine 52 °F from January 1), whose source, species and temperature basis aren't known (not UC IPM's page, now or in 2014), checked only against VDIFN; this report replaces that one.

**In short:** egg hatch of the northern corn rootworm as Apple, Walgenbach and Knee 1971 measured it in Wisconsin (base 52 °F; first hatch about 300, median 533 degree days in air, all hatched by about 1,000), larvae feeding on roots for about 350 degree days after hatch (the risk), and beetles half out at about 1,075, from UW–Madison's 1,300 DD50. The hatch follows Apple et al.'s buried eggs closely (35, 79 and 98 % at the totals where they had 37, 81 and 95 %). On the normals half the eggs hatch around June 14 at Arlington, a week after central Iowa's average peak, and the larvae peak in late June, a week or two before UW–Madison's early to mid-July. The later stages run early by the papers now read: western corn rootworm larvae take 371–478 degree days in the laboratory (the model gives 350), and in New York fields half the beetles emerged at about 1,330–1,380 (the model has 1,075). The air totals stand in for soil temperatures, which the eggs and larvae live in; no soil feature is built.

## Choices and differences

- **Species:** the timing is the northern corn rootworm's (_Diabrotica barberi_), the species Apple et al. studied. The western corn rootworm's (_D. virgifera virgifera_) eggs need more: half hatched at 380–426 °C·d (684–767 °F·d) above 11.1 °C in the laboratory studies Davis et al. cite, against Apple et al.'s 529.9 °F·d for Wisconsin's northern eggs. Read as air totals the way Apple et al.'s 530 matched their field eggs, that would put western hatch 9 to 14 days after the model's at Arlington (June 23–28 on the normals, against June 14). Iowa State notes northern males begin emerging 5–7 days after western ones. The larvae can't be told apart in the field, and one model serves both.
- **Degree days:** single sine above 52 °F from January 1 with no upper threshold (stored). Apple et al. used official air temperatures recorded a mile from the plot and don't say how they computed degree days; their Table 3 seems to count from the eggs' burial on April 15. The single sine is UC IPM's recommendation. Arlington's normal sine total reaches 47 by April 15, and a daily average (the usual method of the time, as Chiang and Sisson used) gives less on cool spring days, so both differences make the model's hatch, if anything, a little early.
- **Air, not soil:** the eggs and larvae live in the soil. Apple et al.'s 304 and 530 were measured at constant temperatures, so they are the eggs' own degree days; their 533 in air is one field season's match to eggs 2 inches down in a plot kept bare, in a spring with 58.3 % of the possible sunshine. Chiang and Sisson found air totals at first hatch about 100 above soil totals and varying with cover: 420–446 over bare soil, about 490 under crop residue, 668 under weeds. The model's numbers are therefore bare-soil numbers; under residue or a crop, hatch would come later. Iowa State's 684–767 can't be set against air totals (above). A soil feature from the hourly soil temperatures wasn't built (Ben, 2026-11-23: a later step).
- **Egg hatch:** a PERT curve (300, 495, 1000), weight 0: 10 % at 388, 50 % at 534 and 90 % at 723. The start is Apple's first hatch; the median their 533; the end their 998, when all the buried eggs had hatched (first set as a judgment, now matched).
- **Larvae feeding on roots:** from hatch for 350 degree days, the risk (weight 1). UW Vegetable Entomology and A3646 give about three weeks; at the normal rate from mid-June to mid-July (15 to 19 a day at Arlington, 12 to 16 at Rhinelander) that's about 350. A judgment, at or below the laboratory's shortest: Jackson and Elliott's western larvae took 371–478 degree days above 52 °F on fresh seedlings at 18–27 °C, and field larvae are likely slower.
- **Beetles:** from 542 after hatch, for 700 (weight 0: silk clipping and egg laying are scouted, not modeled as risk). XHT1087's 1,300 DD50 is reached on the normals on the days the total above 52 °F reaches 1,094 (Arlington, July 15), 1,073 (Hancock, July 19) and 1,054 (Rhinelander, July 29); read as half the beetles out, at about 1,075, 542 after the median hatch. What's left after the larvae, 192 (about 11 days in July), is the pupa, as Jackson and Elliott's 167–195. Hatch to beetle is 542, below Jackson and Elliott's 568–712. The beetles' 700 (about six weeks) is a judgment.
- **One generation:** the eggs laid in August hatch the next spring. Extended diapause and the soybean-laying variant don't change the timing.
- **Window:** "Dig and rate roots for larval injury", from half the larvae done feeding to 95 % (885–1,123): UW Vegetable Entomology and A3646 advise digging in July or August, before root decay hides the injury.
- **Against VDIFN:** VDIFN's window (329 to 636) put the end of larval feeding in late June. Its numbers turn out to be Davis et al.'s Mt. Pleasant, New York, totals in °C·d above 11 °C, used as °F·d, which put every stage far too early.

## Checks

### Against its sources' numbers

`packages/agmodels/tests/test_beetles_and_plant_bug.py`, run in CI:

- Hatch begins at 300, against Apple et al.'s first hatch at 304 in the laboratory, and half the eggs have hatched at 533 degree days above 52 °F (534 on the grid), the air total on June 13, 1970, when buried eggs reached median hatch.
- Half the beetles are out at 1,075 (533 + 542), within 1,054 to 1,094, the normals' totals above 52 °F on the days they reach XHT1087's 1,300 DD50.
- The larvae feed for 350 after hatch (UW's three weeks); the pupa takes the remaining 192.
- One generation; the window runs from 885 to 1,123.

### Against larvae and beetles in New York fields

Davis, Brenes and Allee 1996 sampled larvae and pupae and caged emerging beetles in 11 central New York cornfields in 1991–1993, and fitted logistic curves to air degree days by the sine method from January 1 above 11 °C (51.8 °F, a fifth of a degree below the model's 52 °F) with no upper threshold, the model's method. The model's beetles come about 13 days before those fields' totals at Arlington (July 14 against July 27), its larvae finish feeding about two weeks before the New York pupae, and the gap opens after hatch: first instars come 83 degree days after the model's half hatched, a few days, as they should, but the larvae take 532 there (first instars at 616 to pupae at 1,148, against the model's 350 from hatch) and the beetles come 712 after the first instars, against the model's 542 from hatch. New York's fields aren't Wisconsin's: Davis et al. found the two research farms 15–28 % apart and Illinois fields needing 1,915 °F·d for half the western beetles, and they put the differences down to soil, local adaptation and the corn's own development. Their normalized model (each day's total over the place's normal January–September total) would put half the western beetles on August 14 at Arlington, later still. The model wasn't changed to match (proposed to Ben, 2026-11-23).

## Limits and open questions

- **The larvae and beetles run early, and the papers now read say why.** The model's larvae peak a week or two before UW–Madison's early to mid-July, its half of the beetles (July 15 at Arlington) comes before the first beetles an old three-year study at Arlington found (July 16–24, UW Vegetable Entomology), and New York's fields put half the beetles 13 days later at Arlington. Four things point the same way: (1) Apple et al.'s eggs were 2 inches deep in bare soil, and deeper eggs and eggs under residue hatch later (Apple et al., p. 855; Chiang and Sisson's air totals at first hatch were 420–446 over bare soil and about 490 under residue); (2) western corn rootworm eggs need about 150–240 more degree days than Apple's northern ones (above); (3) the larvae's 350 is at or below Jackson and Elliott's laboratory 371–478 on ideal food; (4) hatch to beetle, 542, is below their 568–712 and well below New York's field 712 from first instars. The page and topic page say the model may run early (Ben, 2026-11-23; no shift). The change proposed to Ben (2026-11-23): larvae about 550 (New York's 532 from first instars to pupae; above the laboratory's 371–478, as field larvae should be) and the pupa about 200 (Jackson and Elliott's 167–195), so hatch to beetle about 750. On Arlington's normal that would put the larvae's peak about 6 days later (around June 30), the root-digging window's opening around July 14 (from July 4), 10 % of the beetles out around July 18, inside the old Arlington study's first beetles (July 16–24), and half around July 25.
- **Air temperatures stand in for the soil** (Ben, 2026-11-23: air degree days stay for now). What the papers say about a soil feature: for **hatch**, soil totals are steadier than air ones (Chiang and Sisson: 395–424 soil degree days at 5–10 cm to first hatch in three years, bare and weedy, against 420–668 in air), and on soil totals Apple et al.'s laboratory 304 and 530 would apply directly. For the **larvae and beetles**, Davis et al. found soil totals at 10 cm no better than air ones in New York (the farms 15–28 % apart on soil), and air totals capped at 18 °C (64.4 °F) best. So a soil feature would help most for hatch, the stage that matters least to a grower (the risk is the larvae, and controls are chosen at planting); it needs a check of ERA5-Land's soil layers (0–7 cm, not 5–10 cm at a station), their lag in the archive and the forecast, and of whether soil totals move the larvae and beetles toward UW–Madison's dates.
- **Western corn rootworm's hatch** isn't separately timed; the laboratory numbers (Wilde 1971; Levine, Oloumi-Sadeghi and Ellis 1992, J. Econ. Entomol. 85: 2425–2432) aren't in hand, and Iowa State's 684–767 are probably theirs.
- **Wisconsin observations:** Apple et al. found the first larvae in corn roots near Madison on June 7–23 in 1965–1970 (median June 15–16), and the 1970 one a day before the buried eggs' median hatch. Those years can't be run (the archive starts in 2016), and the climate was cooler; on the 1996–2025 normals the model has 10 % hatched by June 4 at Arlington. No recent Wisconsin counts of hatch or larvae are known to us.
