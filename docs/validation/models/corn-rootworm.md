# Corn rootworm (`corn-rootworm`): validation

Model version 0.3, written 2026-11-21 (D98; part H, batch H4), its sources read in full 2026-11-23, and the larvae and pupa lengthened the same day (0.3) from the papers read, with Wilde 1971, Levine, Oloumi-Sadeghi and Ellis 1992 and the original XHT1087 handout read too. The model: `packages/agmodels/src/agmodels/insects/corn_rootworm.py`, run by the insect engine (`agmodels.phenology`, D36); its page: `corn_rootworm.md` beside it. Version 0.1 was VDIFN's window (329, 472, 636 degree days, single sine 52 °F from January 1), whose source, species and temperature basis aren't known (not UC IPM's page, now or in 2014), checked only against VDIFN; this report replaces that one.

**In short:** egg hatch of the northern corn rootworm as Apple, Walgenbach and Knee 1971 measured it in Wisconsin (base 52 °F; first hatch about 300, median 533 degree days in air, all hatched by about 1,000), larvae feeding on roots for about 550 degree days after hatch (the risk; Davis, Brenes and Allee 1996's New York fields), a pupa of about 200 (Jackson and Elliott 1988), and so beetles half out at about 1,283. The hatch follows Apple et al.'s buried eggs closely (35, 79 and 98 % at the totals where they had 37, 81 and 95 %), and western corn rootworm's medians under changing temperatures (509–650) fall inside it, so one hatch serves both species. On the normals half the eggs hatch around June 14 at Arlington, the larvae peak around July 3 (UW–Madison: early to mid-July), the first beetles come around July 15, the day the normals reach UW–Madison's 1,300 DD50 for adult beetles, and 10 % are out by July 18, inside the old Arlington study's first beetles (July 16–24). Version 0.2 had the larvae at 350 and hatch to beetle at 542, which put the larvae's peak in late June and half the beetles out by the first beetles' dates. The air totals stand in for soil temperatures, which the eggs and larvae live in; no soil feature is built.

## Choices and differences

- **Species:** the hatch is the northern corn rootworm's (_Diabrotica barberi_), the species Apple et al. studied; it serves the western corn rootworm (_D. virgifera virgifera_) too, without a form or spread of its own. Under changing temperatures, western eggs were half hatched at 509 °F·d above 51.8 °F (South Dakota, Fisher 1989) to 650 (Illinois, Levine et al.'s soil chamber), June 13 to 21 on Arlington's normal against the model's June 14; the model has 43 to 78 % hatched by those totals. The constant-temperature medians are higher (Wilde's Minnesota eggs 656–850 at 20–25 °C, Levine et al.'s Illinois eggs 767), but Levine et al.'s own eggs hatched with about 17 % fewer degree days under changing temperatures than their constant-temperature fit, and Apple et al.'s northern eggs matched their laboratory 530 in the field only in bare soil. Levine et al. found populations differ (northern ones hatch sooner), so a Wisconsin western population would likely fall between South Dakota's and Illinois's. The spread agrees too: Levine et al.'s hatch took 8 days from 10 to 50 % and 10 days from 50 to 90 %, the model's 10 and 11 on Arlington's normal, and Missouri's field hatch about a month. Version 0.2's report read Davis et al.'s "380–426" as °C·d to half hatched for both papers and put western hatch 9 to 14 days after the model's; Wilde's 380 is °F·d to first hatch, and that gap was a unit slip. Iowa State notes northern males begin emerging 5–7 days after western ones; the larvae can't be told apart in the field.
- **Degree days:** single sine above 52 °F from January 1 with no upper threshold (stored). Apple et al. used official air temperatures recorded a mile from the plot and don't say how they computed degree days; their Table 3 seems to count from the eggs' burial on April 15. The single sine is UC IPM's recommendation. Arlington's normal sine total reaches 47 by April 15, and a daily average (the usual method of the time, as Chiang and Sisson used) gives less on cool spring days, so both differences make the model's hatch, if anything, a little early.
- **Air, not soil:** the eggs and larvae live in the soil. Apple et al.'s 304 and 530 were measured at constant temperatures, so they are the eggs' own degree days; their 533 in air is one field season's match to eggs 2 inches down in a plot kept bare, in a spring with 58.3 % of the possible sunshine. Chiang and Sisson found air totals at first hatch about 100 above soil totals and varying with cover: 420–446 over bare soil, about 490 under crop residue, 668 under weeds. The model's numbers are therefore bare-soil numbers; under residue or a crop, hatch would come later. Iowa State's 684–767 can't be set against air totals (above). A soil feature from the hourly soil temperatures wasn't built (Ben, 2026-11-23: a later step).
- **Egg hatch:** a PERT curve (300, 495, 1000), weight 0: 10 % at 388, 50 % at 534 and 90 % at 723. The start is Apple's first hatch; the median their 533; the end their 998, when all the buried eggs had hatched (first set as a judgment, now matched).
- **Larvae feeding on roots:** from hatch for 550 degree days, the risk (weight 1). Davis et al.'s New York fields took 532 from half the first instars (616 °F·d) to half the pupae (1,148), and field larvae should take more than Jackson and Elliott's western larvae on fresh seedlings at constant 18–27 °C (371–478). At the normal rate from mid-June to mid-July (18 a day at Arlington, 15 at Rhinelander) that's about a month, longer than the three weeks UW Vegetable Entomology and A3646 give each larva; version 0.2 took those three weeks (350), which put the larvae's peak in late June, before UW's early to mid-July.
- **Beetles:** from 750 after hatch, for 700 (weight 0: silk clipping and egg laying are scouted, not modeled as risk). The larvae's 550 and a pupa of 200 (Jackson and Elliott's 167–195 in the laboratory; New York's fields 180–233 from half the pupae to half the northern and western beetles, 1,148 to 1,328 and 1,381). Hatch to beetle, 750, is above Jackson and Elliott's 568–712 on ideal food and near New York's 712 from half the first instars to half the northern beetles. Half the beetles are out at about 1,283 and 10 % at 1,138. XHT1087's "adult beetles present at 1300 DD50" is reached on the normals on the days the total above 52 °F reaches 1,094 (Arlington, July 15), 1,073 (Hancock, July 19) and 1,054 (Rhinelander, July 29), when 0 to 3 % of the model's beetles are out: the first beetles, as "present" reads. Version 0.2 read it as half the beetles out, 542 after the median hatch. The beetles' 700 (about six weeks) is a judgment.
- **One generation:** the eggs laid in August hatch the next spring. Extended diapause and the soybean-laying variant don't change the timing.
- **Window:** "Dig and rate roots for larval injury", from half the larvae done feeding to 95 % (1,085–1,323; 885–1,123 in 0.2): UW Vegetable Entomology and A3646 advise digging in July or August, before root decay hides the injury.
- **Against VDIFN:** VDIFN's window (329 to 636) put the end of larval feeding in late June. Its numbers turn out to be Davis et al.'s Mt. Pleasant, New York, totals in °C·d above 11 °C, used as °F·d, which put every stage far too early.

## Limits and open questions

- **The larvae and pupa are built (0.3, Ben, 2026-11-23).** Version 0.2's larvae (350) and hatch to beetle (542) ran early: its larvae peaked a week or two before UW–Madison's early to mid-July and half its beetles were out by the first beetles' dates at Arlington. The larvae now take 550 (New York's fields) and the pupa 200 (Jackson and Elliott), and the first beetles fall on XHT1087's 1,300 DD50 and inside Arlington's July 16–24. What remains: the larvae's 550 comes from New York's fields, with one check against them that isn't independent; each larva now takes about a month in July, against UW–Madison's "about three weeks" (which may describe a larva's feeding at warmer July temperatures, or be a round figure); and no Wisconsin counts of larvae, pupae or emerging beetles are known to us to check the stages after hatch. Apple et al.'s eggs were 2 inches deep in bare soil, and deeper eggs and eggs under residue hatch later (Apple et al., p. 855; Chiang and Sisson's air totals at first hatch were 420–446 over bare soil and about 490 under residue), which would move every stage later in many fields.
- **Air temperatures stand in for the soil** (Ben, 2026-11-23: air degree days stay for now). What the papers say about a soil feature: for **hatch**, soil totals are steadier than air ones (Chiang and Sisson: 395–424 soil degree days at 5–10 cm to first hatch in three years, bare and weedy, against 420–668 in air), and on soil totals Apple et al.'s laboratory 304 and 530 would apply directly. For the **larvae and beetles**, Davis et al. found soil totals at 10 cm no better than air ones in New York (the farms 15–28 % apart on soil), and air totals capped at 18 °C (64.4 °F) best. So a soil feature would help most for hatch, the stage that matters least to a grower (the risk is the larvae, and controls are chosen at planting); it needs a check of ERA5-Land's soil layers (0–7 cm, not 5–10 cm at a station), their lag in the archive and the forecast, and of whether soil totals move the larvae and beetles toward UW–Madison's dates.
- **Western corn rootworm's hatch** isn't separately timed (above: its medians under changing temperatures fall inside the model's hatch). Populations differ (Levine et al.), and no study of Wisconsin's western eggs is known to us. Iowa State's and the University of Minnesota's 684–767 "soil degree days" for half hatched carry Davis et al.'s slip (684 is Wilde's 380 °F·d to first hatch, times 1.8) and are constant-temperature numbers, not soil totals; worth telling them.
- **Prolonged diapause:** a small share of western eggs (0.1–0.2 %, Levine et al.) and many northern eggs in some places (the rotation-resistant variant) hatch after a second winter, in June as usual; it doesn't change the timing.
- **Wisconsin observations:** Apple et al. found the first larvae in corn roots near Madison on June 7–23 in 1965–1970 (median June 15–16), and the 1970 one a day before the buried eggs' median hatch. Those years can't be run (the archive starts in 2016), and the climate was cooler; on the 1996–2025 normals the model has 10 % hatched by June 4 at Arlington. No recent Wisconsin counts of hatch or larvae are known to us.
